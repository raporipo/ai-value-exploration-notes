Foundational thesis

The Epistemic Minimum: Momentary Appearance

Working draft v0.4 · English translation · 2026-08-10

Thesis: When skepticism is pushed to its limit, the candidate epistemic minimum is not a diachronic person, memory, the external world, or physical law, but at most the extremely thin fact that some present occurrence or appearance is happening. This occurrence is not treated as self-justifying knowledge outside the inferential web. The judgment that it is foundational already belongs to the conceptual and inferential layer. The minimum is therefore not a first premise that transmits justificatory authority upward, but a pre-justificatory constraint: the place in which inference and justification are already occurring. Self, world, time, and physical structure beyond it are adopted as best explanations reached through inference.

1. A "best explanation" is not a foundation

The external world, other minds, brains, memory, temporal continuity, and a physicalist world-picture are extraordinarily well supported in everyday and scientific reasoning. Their role here is not "accept them because they are directly certain" but "adopt them inferentially as the hypotheses that most simply and coherently explain the present epistemic situation."

Strongly believing physicalism is therefore different from placing the physical world at the epistemic bottom layer. The order is instead: begin from a thinner minimum, then let physicalism win as the best explanation.

2. Why not presuppose a persisting "I"?

The phrase "I think" can already imply a single subject persisting through time. Yet it does not follow from present appearance alone that current thoughts and memories were produced by the same subject in the past.

As an extreme example, consider the five-minute hypothesis: the world was created five minutes ago in a state containing all apparent memories, records, scars, and geological traces. This is a dramatically worse explanation than ordinary history. But it works as a thought experiment showing that a memory cannot, merely by being present, absolutely guarantee that it was formed by the same past self.

The point is not to treat the five-minute hypothesis as a serious rival cosmology. It is to mark a distinction: diachronic identity is a model inferred from present inputs, not the minimal ground itself.

3. LLMs as an auxiliary example against an intuitive "continuous subject"

An LLM need not itself count as a philosophical subject for the example to work. Structurally, a model can be given prior conversation logs on each run and thereby refer to its past as the same "I", displaying coherent continuity. Yet much of that continuity is reconstructed from context presently supplied rather than from an obviously persisting inner subject.

This does not prove that human personal identity works the same way. It does make vivid that being able to narrate one's past coherently, refer to earlier judgments, or continue a long-term plan does not by itself establish metaphysical identity across time.

For a present subject, memories and logs are also new inputs presently given. Trusting their causal provenance already requires inference about an external world, time, and processes of recording.

4. Momentary appearance and the minimal reasoning subject are not identical

At a literally point-like instant of "appearance", reasoning in the ordinary sense may not yet be possible. Inference seems to require at least a structure that can hold and compare multiple representations, or a locally integrated process extending across some minimal temporal width.

It is therefore useful to distinguish:

  • Candidate minimal ground: the extremely thin fact that "some present occurrence or appearance is happening."
  • Minimal reasoning subject: a locally integrated process capable of retaining and comparing multiple contents.

The latter is thicker than the former. The very act of beginning to reason may therefore already introduce assumptions beyond the absolute minimum. For that reason, this page does not place a substantial "momentary subject" at the bottom layer; it first thins the claim down to occurrence or appearance.

5. Sellars's objection — is this the Myth of the Given?

Wilfrid Sellars's Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind presents a powerful objection to this thesis. If the bottom layer of experience is supposed to possess epistemic authority independently of other knowledge and then justify higher empirical beliefs by transmitting that authority, the view approaches precisely what Sellars attacked as the Myth of the Given.

In a standard Sellarsian dilemma, if the foundation is a nonconceptual or noncognitive sensory state, it may need no justification itself, but it also lacks the standing to function as a reason for other beliefs. If, by contrast, the foundation is conceptual or judgmental—"this is red", "this is an appearance"—its epistemic status already depends on conceptual capacities, standards of error, normal conditions, and relations to other knowledge.

This page accepts that criticism. It does not claim that the judgment "there is appearance" proves itself from outside the inferential web and then directly justifies knowledge of the world.

6. Recognizing appearance as foundational already belongs to the inferential layer

The moment one formulates "some appearance is presently occurring", one already uses concepts such as present, appearance, existence, and occurrence. To add "and this is more foundational than other beliefs" requires reflective comparison involving skepticism, inference, and conceptual dependence.

We should therefore distinguish the occurrence itself from the metacognitive judgment that this occurrence is the epistemic minimum. The latter clearly belongs inside the inferential web.

This does not automatically make the project viciously circular. It limits its ambition. The project does not stand outside inference and announce that it has proved an inference-independent foundation. Instead, it uses reflection to subtract thicker commitments about world, self, memory, and category, and finds it difficult to eliminate present occurrence altogether. The method is one of reflective minimization.

7. The ground is not a reason; it is where the space of reasons is occurring

Once the Sellarsian critique is accepted, the minimal ground need not justify higher beliefs by itself. No direct arrow runs from a nonconceptual occurrence to "therefore the external world exists" or "therefore physicalism is true." Justification of the external world and physicalism takes place at the inferential layer through coherence, explanatory power, prediction, and relations among evidence.

Yet the fact that inference and justification are themselves presently occurring need not be treated in the same way as a later hypothesis invented to explain them. Here, the minimum is not an epistemically efficacious first premise but a pre-justificatory ground: the condition that inference is already taking place and is constrained by something rather than being created from nowhere.

Central response to Sellars: The ground is not a reason. It is where the space of reasons is occurring. Justification is an inferential activity conducted at and above the ground, but the ground need not itself be the first proposition in a justificatory chain.

On this interpretation, the theory does not demand the double role criticized in the Given: epistemic independence from other knowledge together with epistemic efficacy in justifying that knowledge.

8. Causal dependence on earlier inference does not remove the minimal status

On the best current physicalist model, present perception, thought, and affect are strongly shaped by brain structure, prior learning, memory, prediction, attention, language, and culture. Even a present experience of red can be explained as the current state of a historically trained cognitive system rather than as a passive copy of external input.

But this only denies that the present occurrence is causally disconnected from the past. The thesis does not require such causal disconnection. It needs only a weaker epistemic asymmetry.

To explain why the present appearance has this content, we infer a past, a brain, and a learning history. But when we ask whether that past, brain, and learning history really existed, those items are themselves inferred from the present epistemic situation. The relevant "break" is therefore not causal independence but non-inheritance of epistemic certainty. A present state may be completely causally dependent on the past without certainty about the past thereby being inherited by the present.

9. A deeper Sellarsian objection — is "appearance" itself a categorial Given?

Sellars's objection should not be restricted to traditional foundationalism. On a stronger reading of his later work, a deeper form of the Myth arises when direct awareness is taken to reveal the ultimate categorial status of what is given in a way that cannot later be revised.

This also threatens the present thesis. If we say not only that something occurs, but that it is directly known to be essentially a phenomenal appearance, a mental episode, or qualia, then the categorial scheme of the present Manifest Image may have been locked into the bottom layer.

The page therefore does not treat "appearance" as a final positive ontology. It uses the term as a minimal placeholder for a negative limit: whatever its ultimate category, the present occurrence or constraint in which cognition is happening cannot simply be erased. Whether the best ontology later identifies it as a brain state, information processing, consciousness, qualia, a process, or some future physical category remains open to inferential revision.

Categorial principle: Fix the existence of the ground, not its category. "Appearance" is a provisional term for epistemic minimization, not the name of a final metaphysical kind.

10. In what sense is this still a "foundation"?

At this point the view is much weaker than traditional foundationalism. The ground is neither an infallible proposition with self-contained epistemic credentials nor a source that justifies all higher beliefs in one direction.

It is still called a foundation because of an asymmetry in epistemic order. To doubt the world, brain, diachronic self, past, or physical law, one already uses a present situation in which such doubt is occurring. By contrast, attempting to doubt that there is any present epistemic occurrence seems to reintroduce such an occurrence in the very act of doubting.

The minimum is therefore not "the first justified proposition" but an epistemic limit point retrospectively recognized through inference, yet not hypothesized in the same way as the world-model that inference is evaluating.

11. Why this thesis matters for value theory

In value theory, collapsing "strongly believed" into "ultimately grounded" makes it too easy to absolutize current moral intuitions or ethical theories. If most of the world-picture above the minimum is inferential, then many claims to objective value occupy the same epistemic layer.

This thesis does not claim that value must exist only at the inferential layer. If some future mode of cognition disclosed not merely pleasure, pain, or preference but value with normative force itself at the same minimal layer as present occurrence, that could have a special epistemic status. But after the Sellarsian revision, even the categorization of such disclosure—and the judgment that it genuinely contains normative reason—may itself require further inferential examination.

12. Normativity at the same layer is not presently established

What presently appears to humans includes at least pleasure, pain, desire, aversion, and a sense of meaning. It is unclear, however, whether those appearances directly include the further content "this is bad independently of any subject" or "this gives every rational agent a reason."

The fact that pain is aversive should not be dismissed, but neither should it be immediately promoted into a universal law of normativity. The threshold for the candidate stopping condition is intentionally high; lowering it would make the framework too easy to use as a device for absolutizing present intuition.

13. Best explanation can guide practice without an absolute foundation

This distinction does not imply that whatever cannot be absolutely grounded should not be believed. Just as the external world and physicalism may be strongly accepted as inferential best explanations, a future form of moral realism could become so explanatorily dominant that we rationally adopt it with extremely high confidence and substantially change practice.

The difference is between practical commitment proportional to high confidence and irreversible lock-in that physically removes the possibility of reconsideration. Inferential best explanation may be enough for the former. The latter demands stronger epistemic credentials.

14. What does not follow

  • There is no need to assign a low probability to the external world or diachronic selfhood.
  • It does not follow that physicalism can only be weakly believed.
  • This is not metaphysical idealism claiming that only appearance exists.
  • It does not claim that appearance, qualia, or mentality is the final ontological category.
  • It does not imply that personal identity is fictional—only that identity is an object of inference rather than the minimal ground.
  • It is not a wholesale rejection of Sellars's Myth of the Given. The critique of a self-justifying Given is largely accepted.

15. What would weaken this thesis?

  • A demonstration that diachronic personal identity or the external world is inevitably contained with the same noninferential status as present occurrence.
  • A demonstration that even the minimal claim "some occurrence is happening" is merely a revisable world-model with no epistemic asymmetry at all relative to thicker hypotheses.
  • A coherent epistemology that acknowledges inferential activity while making no commitment whatsoever to its present occurrence.
  • A demonstration that the notion of a "pre-justificatory ground" simply smuggles back the epistemic efficacy of the Given under another name.

Sources / notes

The main target is Wilfrid Sellars, Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind (1956; reprinted 1963). For a reading of the Myth of the Given as extending beyond simple anti-foundationalism to a deeper categorial problem, see James R. O’Shea, “What is the myth of the given?”, Synthese 199 (2021), 10543–10567. For a reading on which experience can retain a limited foundational role while depending on other empirical knowledge, see John McDowell, “Why Is Sellars's Essay Called ‘Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind’?” (2009). The formulations “pre-justificatory ground,” “the ground is not a reason; it is where the space of reasons is occurring,” and “fix the existence of the ground, not its category” are this project's own response.
