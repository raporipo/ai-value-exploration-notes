Foundational thesis

Value Structure — Content, Normative Bridges, and Reason Structure

Value inquiry is not only inquiry into a list of things that are good. It also asks what is normatively relevant, why it gives reasons to whom, and how those reasons guide action.

Working thesis v0.1 · 2026-08-26

Thesis: This project treats action-guiding “value” not as a single evaluative content but as an interdependent theoretical package involving at least content C, normative bridges B, and reason or normative structure R. Provisionally, call this a value structure 𝒱 = (C, B, R). We leave open both the possibility that present human thought has strong evidence about some components and the possibility that one or all components remain unconceived.

1. “What matters?” is not yet a complete account of reasons

Pain provides a simple example. It is one question whether pain has a negative evaluative feature; another whether that fact gives the sufferer a reason to avoid it; another whether it gives other agents reasons to reduce it; another how reasons across agents compare or aggregate; and another whether reasons generate obligations, permissions, prohibitions, or coercive authority. Treating all of these as one undifferentiated question about “value” hides where additional argument is needed.

2. Three working components

C — content / evaluative or normatively relevant content
What is positive, negative, important, protected, or otherwise potentially relevant to reasons: pain, welfare, freedom, persons, promises, relationships, forms of action, or features not yet conceptualized. C alone does not yet determine who has what reason.
B — normative bridge
The relation by which content or other facts about the world give reasons to some subject. Questions about moving from phenomenal disvalue to agent-independent reasons, or from symmetry to universal reasons, belong here.
R — reason or normative structure
The structure governing the strength, scope, competition, priority, aggregation, distribution, obligation, permission, prohibition, and sometimes coercive implications of reasons.

This is an analytic decomposition, not a claim that the world contains three independent kinds of entities. The boundary between B and R is itself provisional.

3. Value structure is not a Cartesian product

𝒱 = (C, B, R) does not mean that arbitrary components may be freely combined. The components can constrain and reshape one another. A new theory of subjects may change which facts count as normatively relevant content; a newly discovered bridge may redescribe previously psychological features as normatively central; a different reason structure may revise the boundaries among value, reason, and obligation. The target is therefore a coherent package that also fits the rest of our understanding of the world.

4. Existing moral thought may be partly right

The absence of a presently established bridge from an existing moral content to normativity does not show that the content is false or that no bridge exists. Humanity may be broadly right about pain, freedom, persons, or promises while lacking the correct account of why these facts generate reasons or how those reasons interact. Future work on consciousness, agency, reasons, ontology, or rationality could connect familiar content to bridges or reason structures not yet known.

Bridge non-discovery ≠ bridge non-existence: Failing to have discovered an adequate bridge does not assign zero possibility to such a bridge.

Candidate packages therefore include mixtures such as (C_existing, B*, R*) as well as more radical (C*, B*, R*) structures.

5. Existing moral theories are packages and partial hypotheses

Utilitarianism, deontology, contractualism, virtue ethics, and other traditions need not be treated only as atomic competitors. A theory may be insightful about C while failing to ground B, or may offer plausible C and B while using the wrong aggregation rule in R. Existing ethics is therefore both a set of candidate packages and a store of observations, concepts, and partial hypotheses about different parts of value structure.

6. Realism and anti-realism may also decompose

The question “does value exist?” may hide several questions. A theory might posit objective evaluative features while treating bridges or reason structure as constructed; a reasons-first theory might make C comparatively thin and treat reason relations as basic; error-theoretic or expressivist accounts remain open possibilities. This thesis therefore does not presuppose moral realism.

7. Reframing value exploration

Value exploration: inquiry that uses evidence and reasoning about the world, experience, consciousness, agency, rationality, society, and the cosmos to discover, compare, criticize, and revise the relations among C, B, and R. It leaves open unconceived content, unconceived bridges and reason structures, mixed known/unknown packages, and theories that revise the C/B/R decomposition itself.

8. Implications for stopping conditions

Strong support for one component does not automatically close all value inquiry. “Pain has negative evaluative significance” might become settled while questions about who has reasons, how strong they are, or how they interact remain open. Conversely, not every ethical question must be solved globally before a local question can close; closure can be question-specific.

9. The decomposition itself remains revisable

This thesis does not claim that the true normative structure must naturally split into C, B, and R. A future theory may collapse value and reasons, absorb B into R, or replace the entire decomposition with concepts unavailable to us now. 𝒱 = (C, B, R) is therefore a fallible meta-theoretical representation for separating argumentative steps that are currently easy to conflate.

10. Implication for the Core

The Core should preserve uncertainty broader than “perhaps there is an unknown true good.” Present human content may be vindicated by a bridge not yet discovered; familiar bridge or reason structures may connect to very different content; or all three may be revised. Preserving explorability therefore means retaining routes by which content, bridges, and reason structure can be redescribed, tested, and criticized—not maximizing exposure to novel value claims.
