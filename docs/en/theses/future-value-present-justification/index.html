Foundational Thesis

Normative Bridges from Future Value to Present Action

Keeping unknown value open is not the same thing as authorizing sacrifice imposed on present subjects.

Foundational thesis v0.1 · 2026-08-17 · promoted from exploration

Thesis: Even if the future may contain major values presently unknown to us, or better knowledge of value, that possibility alone does not establish authority to impose burdens, sacrifice, restrictions, or coercion on present subjects. Each transition in the sequence value possibility → pro tanto reason → permission → obligation → coercive authorization requires an additional normative bridge, and those bridges must themselves be fallibly justified by the evidence and reasoning presently available.

1. Why this is a Foundational Thesis

The Core argues that while our understanding of value remains inferential and fallible, we should avoid irreversibly closing the routes by which value could later be discovered, criticized, or corrected. But if the mere possibility of unknown future value could override every present interest, right, or claim to autonomy, preservation of explorability could easily mutate into an unlimited demand for sacrifice from present subjects.

This thesis is therefore not merely an application outside the Core. It is an internal constraint that helps keep the Core from collapsing into Pascalian or future-value-dominating reasoning.

2. Separate five normative layers

Value possibility
Major value, or better knowledge of value, may exist in the future.
Pro tanto reason
There is some reason not to ignore that possibility entirely.
Permission
After comparison with other reasons, an action may permissibly be taken.
Obligation
The action ought to be taken.
Coercive authorization
The burden may permissibly be imposed even on present subjects who object.
Non-identity: possibility ≠ reason ≠ permission ≠ obligation ≠ coercive authorization. Moving to each later stage requires further argument.

For example, the possibility of unknown future value may provide some reason to preserve records or fund research. It does not, by itself, establish that present subjects may be deprived by force of bodily security, property, political exit, or independent agency.

3. Future value cannot bootstrap its present authority

The argument “if the future contains enormous true value, that value matters more than present sacrifice, therefore we may impose the sacrifice now” already contains substantive normative premises. These may include principles such as maximizing total value, refusing temporal discounting merely because a subject is future, aggregating present and future claims in a particular way, or allowing opportunities to discover unknown value to override present rights.

Those principles may be defensible candidates. But they do not follow from the possibility of future value itself. Unknown value cannot simultaneously transmit, from the future into the present, the rule specifying how strongly it should override current claims.

Bridge requirement: Connecting future value to present action always requires some bridge principle. The epistemic status of that principle remains subject to scrutiny, counterevidence, and revision just like other present normative judgments.

4. The Core is not normatively neutral

This constraint does not imply that nothing should be done for the future. The Core already contains a thin conditional meta-attitude: if our objectives are justifiable, we want objective choice and revision to remain responsive to that justification. It also gives some weight to corrigibility, information value, and irreversible option loss.

That thin commitment can fairly directly support low-cost preservation of records, continued dissent, basic research, multiple paths of inquiry, and reversible experimentation. But the strength of those reasons must be kept separate from the strength of any authority to impose major burdens on present subjects.

5. Coercion requires a higher additional threshold

Policies intended to preserve explorability require different levels of justification depending on their cost and intrusion on agency.

  • Low-cost and non-coercive: records, backups, research, protection of dissent, and maintaining multiple models. These are relatively easy to support from the thin meta-preference.
  • Moderate burdens: public research funding, redundant infrastructure, and some safety regulation. These require comparison with present welfare, fairness, risk reduction, and other reasons.
  • Major coercion or irreversible intervention: severe restrictions on liberty, elimination of subjects, closure of political exit, or forced fixation of value. The possibility of future value is insufficient; strong content-dependent and procedural justification is required.
Asymmetric burden: Proposals that invoke unknown value while irreversibly closing present subjects’ independent routes of survival, correction, or exit carry an especially high burden of proof.

6. This does not permanently lock in present values

Giving serious weight to present rights, welfare, and autonomy does not establish them as cosmically final values. Present values can be strongly adopted as the best normative evidence and practice currently available while their inferential grounds remain open to future criticism.

This thesis rejects two extremes: “because future value is unknown, present subjects may be sacrificed without limit,” and “because present values are the only values we presently know, future corrigibility may be ignored completely.”

7. Relation to the stopping condition

Conditions for Ending Value Inquiry asks when inquiry may be reduced or stopped. This thesis asks how far a judgment about stopping or continuing may be imposed on others. The questions are related but not identical.

If confidence in a value theory becomes extremely high, it may be rational to follow it strongly and reduce resources devoted to inquiry. But an additional bridge is still needed to move from that confidence to the claim that dissenters, records, or independent routes of verification may all be eliminated.

8. Implications for AI and civilizational design

When advanced AI systems or safety institutions invoke “enormous future value” or “long-run civilizational optimization” to govern present subjects, the five layers above should be kept explicit. Large expected value in a model must not be treated as identical to authorization for irreversible centralized coercive power.

For both AI safety and value exploration, strength of reason, permission to act, obligation, and coercive authority should be calibrated separately. Even when temporary restrictions are justified for safety, their scope, duration, auditability, and routes for exit and reconsideration require independent justification.

9. Conditions for weakening or abandoning the thesis

  • A persuasive foundational theory derives coercive authority directly from value possibility without any additional normative premise.
  • Robust convergence across otherwise plausible normative theories shows that present rights, claims, and autonomy are fully subordinate to future value in all relevant cases.
  • Conversely, if no bridge from future value to present reasons can be justified at all, this thesis would strengthen from “additional bridges are required” to “future value supplies no present reason.”

10. Detailed comparisons and stress tests
