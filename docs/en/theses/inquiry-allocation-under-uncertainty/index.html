Foundational Thesis

Allocating Inquiry Under Unresolved Normative Uncertainty

An unresolved question need not receive maximal inquiry resources. And declining to investigate now does not imply that future investigation may be made impossible.

Foundational thesis v0.1 · 2026-08-20

Thesis: An agent whose objective justification remains unresolved has reason to distinguish and allocate among active inquiry, preservation of inquiry options, and realization of the presently best-supported values, rather than maximizing inquiry as a single activity. The allocation should respond at least to how decision-relevant new information could be to objective justification, how irreversible or non-substitutable the inquiry opportunity is, the risks and opportunity costs of active inquiry, and the cost of preserving the ability to inquire later.

1. Unresolved does not mean high-priority now

Conditions for Ending Value Inquiry asks when a value question may be epistemically closed. But failure to meet a terminal stopping condition does not imply that large present resources should be devoted to value inquiry.

A question can remain unresolved while marginal information is unlikely, the same inquiry can be performed later, inquiry is dangerous, or urgent present harms demand attention. Conversely, an inquiry with modest immediate prospects may deserve preservation if the relevant object or evidence will disappear permanently unless protected now.

Key distinction: unresolved ≠ high-priority now. Epistemically keeping a question open is distinct from maximizing current active inquiry.

2. Three allocations: E / P / X

E — Active inquiry
Perform research, reasoning, experiments, observation, dialogue across different agents, or cognitive enhancement now in order to obtain new information relevant to objective justification.
P — Preservation of inquiry options
Preserve information, unmodified objects, dissent, cognitive diversity, self-revision capacity, computational resources, and access paths so that inquiry can be performed later even if it is not performed now.
X — Present-value realization
Act on the presently best-supported world model and provisional objectives: reduce suffering, manage hazards, sustain institutions, produce goods, and otherwise pursue current practical aims.

The crucial point is that E and P are not identical. Active inquiry may be expensive or dangerous while preserving the possibility of later inquiry is cheap. In that case there is a middle strategy: reduce inquiry now while preserving a future in which inquiry remains possible.

3. Four directional gradients

This thesis does not propose a complete scalar utility function. Giving a single numerical objective from the outset could prejudge the very question of which objective is justified. Instead it proposes at least four directional relationships.

3.1 Greater justificatory relevance strengthens the case for inquiry

The more an item of information could change the agent's judgment about what it should aim at, or whether objective justification is possible at all, the stronger the reason to obtain it. Information largely irrelevant to objective justification receives no special inquiry priority from the Core merely because it is unknown.

3.2 More irreversible loss of an inquiry opportunity strengthens the case for present inquiry or preservation

An object that can be investigated under the same conditions a thousand years from now differs from an object that will be irretrievably destroyed today. In the latter case, there is stronger reason either to investigate now or at least to preserve data, samples, environments, and dissent.

3.3 Higher cost and survival risk weaken active inquiry

Inquiry consumes time and computation, can crowd out present-value realization, burden other agents, require hazardous experiments, or threaten the survival of the inquiring agent or civilization. If inquiry itself is likely to destroy the possibility of future inquiry, there is reason to reduce E.

3.4 Lower preservation cost strengthens P

Sometimes it is cheap to keep records, retain a small number of dissenting agents, preserve unmodified samples, or avoid disabling self-revision. In such cases P can partially substitute for E.

Directional rule: Higher justificatory relevance and greater irreversibility of lost inquiry opportunities strengthen reasons for E or P; higher danger and opportunity cost weaken E; low preservation cost can justify keeping P high while E is low.

4. X remains legitimate

Unresolved objective justification does not eliminate the need to act. Delaying relief of suffering, safety measures, social maintenance, or other practical work has costs of its own. This thesis does not require civilization to stop production until value is settled.

But as Reflective Uncertainty and Irreversible Commitment argues, using a present objective as a provisional action default differs from using it to erase all future capacity for correction. The practical strength of X should therefore be separated from the strength of irreversible lock-in performed in the name of X.

5. Allocation patterns

Preservation relatively cheapPreservation also costly
Inquiry relatively safe and cheapPerform E while retaining PConcentrate selectively on inquiry with high justificatory relevance
Inquiry dangerous or costlyReduce E and prioritize PStrong tradeoffs among present value, survival, and inquiry; conditional suspension may be justified

The lower-left case is especially important. Danger from inquiry need not mean that the inquiry question is settled. The question can remain open while inquiry is placed into reversible dormancy until safer conditions arise.

6. Terminal closure versus practical suspension

Terminal closure
The question itself is settled—for example, foundational normativity is presented with sufficient clarity to the agent's maximal reasoning capacity, or the existence or impossibility of the relevant normativity is logically closed by sufficiently strong proof.
Conditional or practical suspension
The question remains unresolved, but current inquiry paths impose overwhelming risk on the agent, civilization, or future capacity to inquire.

The latter is not an answer. It is a dormant state that can be reopened if conditions change, ideally with P preserved.

7. This does not make explorability an ultimate good

The thesis does not introduce inquiry or reversibility as new ultimate values and does not recommend maximizing inquiry without limit. Its narrower claim is that an agent that has not closed the question of objective justification faces costs on both sides: erasing future correction paths can be costly, and failing to realize present values can also be costly. Allocation should therefore respond to justificatory relevance, irreversibility, danger, and opportunity cost.

8. Implications for advanced AI

For advanced AI, both total devotion of computation to value inquiry and permanent fixation of the current objective are extreme designs. A more flexible agent could act strongly on its present best objective while estimating how decision-relevant further inquiry may be, avoiding dangerous inquiry, and preserving inexpensive channels for reconsideration.

Self-revision capacity, independent reasoning systems, external records, and access to unmodified data can be treated as design variables distinct from active inquiry. In that sense the problem is not only safe exploration of the world, but safe inquiry by an agent whose own objective remains under justificatory review.

9. Relation to prior theory

  • MacAskill, Bykvist & Ord, “Moral Information” analyzes the value of obtaining moral information under moral uncertainty using expected choice-worthiness. This thesis likewise emphasizes decision relevance, but foregrounds cases where the justified objective or evaluative scale itself may still be unsettled.
  • Arrow & Fisher, “Environmental Preservation, Uncertainty, and Irreversibility” (1974) analyzes the informational and option loss associated with irreversible development under uncertainty and future learning. P is close to extending this structure from environmental options to options for future value inquiry and objective correction.
  • Robust Decision Making emphasizes robust, adaptive strategies across multiple plausible futures under deep uncertainty rather than optimization to a single forecast. Here the uncertainty can be deeper still: the normative performance criterion itself may be unsettled.
  • Moldovan & Abbeel, “Safe Exploration in Markov Decision Processes” (2012) studies exploration subject to safety constraints where exploration can damage the system. Here the target of inquiry includes not only the external environment but the justification of the agent's own objective.
Positioning note: The nearest families are value of information, irreversibility and option value, robust adaptation, and safe exploration. The distinctive target here is a case where the objective function used to evaluate those tradeoffs is itself under justificatory uncertainty.

10. Connection to preservation and production civilizations

Preservation vs. Production Civilizations can be read as the civilization-scale version of E/P/X allocation.

Even where X should be large, it does not follow that every unmodified environment, culture, agent type, foundational record, or cosmic resource should be irreversibly transformed according to current values. Yet P also has opportunity costs and cannot automatically override relief of suffering or other present-value realization.

The civilization-scale question is therefore not simply “preserve or produce?” but which objects, at what times, should receive how much E, P, and X?

11. What this thesis does not claim

  • Every unresolved question deserves large present inquiry resources.
  • Explorability or reversibility is an ultimate objective value.
  • Present-value realization is inferior to value inquiry.
  • Inquiry should continue even when it threatens survival.
  • Every datum, culture, ecosystem, or agent should be preserved forever.
  • A single numerical formula can universally determine E/P/X allocation.

12. What would weaken this thesis?

  • Showing that there is no practical distinction among active inquiry, preservation of inquiry options, and present-value realization when objective justification remains unresolved.
  • Showing a sufficiently general principle that always requires maximal inquiry or always requires maximal present-value realization independently of objective justification.
  • Showing that irreversibility or non-substitutability of inquiry opportunities supplies no additional reason in decisions about objective justification.
  • Showing that low-cost P is generally unavailable and that preserving inquiry options always causes equal or greater irreversible loss of present value.
  • Showing that ordinary expected utility or value-of-information methods fully handle cases where the evaluative scale itself is unsettled, without additional assumptions.
