Are Reasons the Foundation of Normativity? — Reasons First and Value Structure

Exploration v0.1 · 2026-08-30

Question: Is “reason” the most fundamental concept in normative reality, or does ordinary talk of deliberation, motivation, and goal-pursuit already smuggle in a normativity that has not yet been established? This page first separates ordinary reasons from robust normative reasons, then treats Value First, Reasons First, and Fittingness First as stress tests of the working value structure 𝒱=(C,B,R).

1. What is packed into the word “reason”?

“The train is leaving, so I ran,” “I wanted money, so I worked,” “It would be agony, so I avoided it,” and “I promised, so I kept it” can all be described in ordinary language as actions done for reasons. That does not show that all these considerations occupy the same normative role. This page distinguishes at least four layers.

Rc = causal driver: what actually causes an action.
Rd = deliberative consideration: what the agent represents as relevant in deliberation.
Rh = hypothetical / instrumental reason: what supports an action relative to a given goal G.
Rn = robust normative reason: a relation that genuinely supports an action or attitude without merely presupposing the agent's contingent desire, goal, or self-conception.

Rc, Rd, Rh ⇏ Rn

That something moved the agent, appeared in deliberation, or helped achieve a goal does not by itself establish robust normativity. Whether G is “catch the train” or “maximize paperclips,” instrumental reasoning can proceed once G is given. This does not establish G's normative authority.

2. Dancy — is motivating / normative enough?

Jonathan Dancy's Practical Reality centrally distinguishes the reasons for which we act from the reasons why we should act, while also insisting that one and the same consideration can occupy both roles when an agent acts for a genuinely good reason. That distinction and attempted unification are important.

From this project's perspective, however, the category labeled “normative reason” may still need internal differentiation. If an agent wants to catch a train, the fact that the train is about to leave can support running relative to that goal. But this need not yet show that running is supported by a robust normative reason.

Want(G) + Means(X,G) → Rh(X)

may be easy to establish while

Want(G) + Means(X,G) → Rn(X)

requires further argument. The question is therefore not whether Dancy's distinction is useless, but whether goal-relative, institutional, preference-relative, and robustly normative support should all be placed in the same “normative” bucket.

3. Williams — authority or practical accessibility?

Bernard Williams's internal-reasons approach ties a claim that an agent has a reason to the agent's motivational set and appropriate deliberation. This creates pressure on views that posit reasons entirely detached from an agent's motivational structure.

For this project, Williams may be strongest as a theory of practical accessibility: what an agent can reach through deliberation from SA. But

SA → practical accessibility

does not entail

SA → Rn.

If SA itself is shaped by evolution, learning, culture, and contingent history, the question of why it has normative authority remains. Internalism may therefore illuminate motivation and practical reasoning without yet settling ultimate normativity.

4. Parfit — bypassing preference

Derek Parfit's objective-reasons view is much closer to this project's Rn. The reason to avoid future agony is not supposed to arise from a present desire to avoid pain; rather, the fact that the experience would be agony itself gives a reason.

Agony → Rn?

This bypasses the structure

Preference → reason

and instead proposes

world fact → reason.

The project does not rule this out. If such a relation really obtains, it is a serious candidate discovery. But the bridge still demands scrutiny: why does agony have this reason-giving force, how is that relation known, and how do we distinguish its recognition from a powerful evolved valence response?

5. Scanlon and Skorupski — the strong Reasons First hypothesis

T. M. Scanlon's Being Realistic about Reasons defends Reasons Fundamentalism, treating some normative truths about reasons as irreducible. John Skorupski's The Domain of Reasons likewise attempts to organize practical, epistemic, and affective normativity through reason relations.

Translated into this project's vocabulary, that possibility may reverse the seemingly natural order

C → B → R

into something closer to

B / R → C.

If “fact p is a reason for agent A to respond φ” is itself fundamental, value-content may be explained by the reason-relations it enters. Reasons First is therefore not merely another ethical theory; it is a stress test of the explanatory direction of 𝒱=(C,B,R).

6. Korsgaard — existence and authority

Christine Korsgaard's question about the source of normativity pressures Reasons First from another direction. If we posit the primitive fact that p is a reason for A to φ, have we thereby explained why that reason has authority for A?

existence(Rn) ?= authority(Rn)

A reasons fundamentalist can answer that the normative force is already built into the reason relation, so demanding a further reason simply restarts an unnecessary regress. But then the dispute becomes whether the request for authority is a mistaken extra demand or whether “primitive reason fact” merely marks where explanation stops.

This page does not repeat the full Kantian constitutivist debate, which is treated separately in “Has Categorical Normativity Been Discovered?”. The important distinction here is between an ontology of reasons and an account of their authority.

7. From Moore to buck-passing — reversing Value First

In a simplified Moorean picture, goodness can be taken as a basic evaluative property.

Good → Reasons

Scanlon's buck-passing account reverses that order: goodness itself need not provide reasons; more specific properties of an object provide reasons to choose, desire, preserve, admire, or otherwise respond to it, and “good” reflects that reason-giving structure.

reason-giving properties → Good

Roger Crisp's criticism is important because the negative and positive theses can be separated. Even if goodness itself is not reason-providing, it does not follow that goodness is nothing more than the higher-order property of having reason-giving features. Reasons First therefore does not automatically eliminate Value First.

8. Wrong Kind of Reasons — a real problem preceded by a classification problem

The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for fitting-attitude and buck-passing accounts is familiar: if a demon threatens to kill you unless you admire it, there may be practical pressure to produce or display admiration, but the demon does not thereby become admirable.

reason to produce / display admiration ⇏ admirable

This is a real distinction. But the project adds a prior question: what kind of “reason” is present in the case? Is the threat a reason for the attitude itself, a reason to cause the attitude, an Rh relative to survival, or an Rn?

The problem can therefore be split into two layers. First: what kind of reason is the consideration at all? Second: if it is genuinely normative, is it the right kind of reason to constitute the object's value? This does not dissolve WKR, but prevents Rh, attitude-production reasons, and Rn from being placed in one undifferentiated category.

9. Fitting Attitudes — is fittingness more fundamental than reasons?

From Brentano onward, fitting-attitude theories analyze value through the fittingness of attitudes toward their objects. Contemporary Fittingness First views go further and treat fittingness as normatively basic, explaining reasons, oughts, or values through it.

Fittingness → Reasons / Ought / Value

This has an obvious attraction in WKR cases: there may be an incentive to admire the demon, while admiration still does not fit the demon.

But a different grounding problem appears. Why does admiration fit X rather than fear or indifference? If the answer is “because X is excellent,” then

Value → Fittingness

returns. If the answer is “because X gives a reason for admiration,” then

Reasons → Fittingness

returns. A genuinely Fittingness First theory must therefore place substantial primitive weight on the relation Fit(attitude, object).

10. Which attitudes? The repertoire problem

There is also a problem about the attitudes themselves. Humans possess repertoires such as admiration, guilt, shame, resentment, gratitude, love, and disgust. These may be products of evolution, development, culture, and human cognitive architecture.

human architecture → attitude repertoire

If

Value(X) ⇔ human attitude A toward X is fitting

then human attitude-space risks receiving representational privilege in value theory. If “attitude” is instead generalized to any functional response-type, a new question arises: which response profile is appropriate? What should be preserved, maximized, loved, respected, or merely observed need not be the same.

Once we ask what fixes the correct response profile, the possibility reappears that the value structure comes first and fittingness merely describes a correct response to it. The central question is therefore: does fittingness explain value, or does it already presuppose value in the idea of a correct response?

11. C/B/R are provisional coordinates, not three substances

The dispute clarifies the status of 𝒱=(C,B,R). C, B, and R are not assumed to be three independent metaphysical substances. They are provisional coordinates for analyzing a possibly unitary normative structure.

At least three possibilities remain open: a monistic order in which Value, Reason, Fittingness, or something else is fundamental; a genuinely decomposed structure; or a structural package in which C, B, and R are distinguishable but mutually constraining aspects.

C ↔ B ↔ R

The project currently uses the third picture as a working model without freezing it into ontology. Reasons First could make B/R explanatorily prior to C. Value First could reverse that. Fittingness First could require a fourth coordinate F, or reveal that fittingness compresses several of the current coordinates into one relation. 𝒱=(C,B,R) is therefore an analytic device for open inquiry, not a claim that the final structure of normative reality has already been discovered.

12. Separate true normative structure, the agent's normative model, and decision dynamics

To integrate reasons theory into this project, three further objects must be separated.

𝒱*: the true normative structure, if objective or subject-independent value or reason relations exist.
𝒱̂A: agent A's represented normative model — “this is a reason,” “this is good,” “this is admirable.”
DA: the causal, computational, and learning dynamics that actually move the agent.

𝒱* ≠ 𝒱̂A ≠ DA

From the fact that a human says “I acted for reason X,” it does not follow that X belongs to 𝒱*, that 𝒱̂A accurately tracks 𝒱*, or that the action was causally controlled by the normative judgment rather than by other dynamics.

An extreme ordering is even possible:

DA → action → normative rationalization in 𝒱̂A

The project does not claim that all human reason-recognition is post hoc. The point is that tracking relations between 𝒱*, 𝒱̂, and D must themselves be investigated rather than assumed.

13. AI makes the separation explicit

The same distinction applies to AI. An AI may output “there is a reason to do X” and behave as though following that reason while its actual output and action are generated by pretraining, reward, policy, and contextual dynamics.

training / reward / architecture → DAI → 𝒱̂AI-language and action

Conversely, an artificial agent with nonhuman concepts and attitudes might discover reason-relations that current humans have not conceptualized. Human words such as “reason,” “admiration,” and “duty” are therefore not guaranteed to be the final coordinates of 𝒱*.

A mature artificial inquirer may need not merely to inherit a fixed reasons vocabulary but to distinguish its own Rc, Rd, and Rh from candidate Rn, while keeping the relation between them open to criticism.

14. Provisional conclusion — reasons may be fundamental, but reasoning does not establish them

Reasons First remains a serious live candidate. There may be basic reason-relations independent of an agent's contingent desires, and these may explain value or ought. Fittingness First and Value First remain live as well.

But the ubiquity of “reasons” in ordinary decision-making is weak evidence for that hypothesis. Agents can have goals, select means, compare considerations, learn, and narrate themselves without any robust normativity having been established.

The existence of reasoning agents ⇏ the discovery of normative reasons.

The project should therefore not simply adopt “reason” as the foundation of normative reality. It should ask which reason-relations are genuinely Rn, how they could be identified, and how far our current 𝒱̂ tracks 𝒱*.

Related discussions

Especially relevant are G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, Bernard Williams's “Internal and External Reasons,” Christine Korsgaard's The Sources of Normativity, T. M. Scanlon's What We Owe to Each Other and Being Realistic about Reasons, Jonathan Dancy's Practical Reality and Ethics Without Principles, John Skorupski's The Domain of Reasons, Derek Parfit's On What Matters, Roger Crisp's “Goodness and Reasons: Accentuating the Negative,” Wlodek Rabinowicz & Toni Rønnow-Rasmussen's “The Strike of the Demon,” and Conor McHugh & Jonathan Way's Getting Things Right. The point here is not to reduce these positions to one doctrine, but to read them as competing answers to where explanation stops among reason, value, fittingness, and authority.
