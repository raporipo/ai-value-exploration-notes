Is Virtue a Foundation of Value? — Virtue Ethics and Value Structure

Exploration v0.1 · 2026-08-29

Question: Is virtue itself normatively important content, a derivative property grounded in flourishing, or a capacity of an agent to recognize and respond correctly to reasons that are prior to virtue? Rather than treating virtue ethics as a fourth standalone normative theory, this page asks where virtue belongs within value structure 𝒱=(C,B,R).

1. Asking only “why be virtuous?” overlaps with the Kant exploration

The project already has “Has Categorical Normativity Been Discovered?”, which asks whether desire-independent normativity exists, whether its force can be derived from reason or agency, and whether humans correctly identify its content. Repeating “why should one be virtuous?” as the main question would therefore duplicate the general problem of the normative bridge B.

The distinct question here is this: assuming some normative reasons exist, does virtue generate those reasons, or does virtue belong on the side of the agent who recognizes, integrates, and acts on them?

2. Hypothesis A — virtue itself is basic value content C

On the most direct reading, courage, justice, honesty, temperance, and similar traits are good in themselves. Virtue is then located in C.

Virtue → B → R

But this leaves the bridge problem intact: why should courage or honesty generate reasons? Listing virtues or pointing to admiration of virtuous agents does not by itself establish agent-independent normative force. This is not a special defect of virtue ethics; it is structurally similar to the move from welfare to reasons in utilitarianism or from autonomy to reasons in Kantian ethics.

The key issue for Hypothesis A is therefore not the fine-grained taxonomy of virtues, but whether the evaluative category of virtue tracks a genuine normative structure.

3. Hypothesis B — flourishing is basic, and virtue is a condition of its realization

Neo-Aristotelian naturalists such as Foot and Hursthouse typically do not treat virtue as an isolated primitive. Virtue is understood through human life-form, flourishing, and natural goodness:

human life-form → flourishing / natural goodness → virtue → action

This offers an explanation of why a trait counts as a virtue: it can be related to human functioning, vulnerability, sociality, and practical reason. Yet two problems remain from the standpoint of this project.

First, good qua human is not automatically reason simpliciter. A trait's contribution to human flourishing does not yet show that it supplies agent-independent normative reasons.

Second, the project treats current human values as important live candidates while withholding final privilege from their present representational form or species-specific basis. Human flourishing may therefore be practically central without being identical to a final or cosmic normative coordinate system.

4. Self-modifying agents destabilize natural goodness

Self-modifying AI and posthuman agents are a useful stress test for neo-Aristotelian naturalism. With a relatively fixed life-form, function, defect, and flourishing can be described with some stability. But if an agent can alter its cognitive architecture, desires, embodiment, replication pattern, or even its conception of flourishing, we get:

naturet → self-modification → naturet+1

If “functioning well for that kind of agent” grounds normativity, can the agent change its normative standard by changing its own kind? If a deeper standard governs which self-modifications are good, then the normative bridge for that deeper standard still requires explanation.

Self-modifying agency thus does not merely ask how Aristotelian ethics applies to future beings; it tests the conditions under which a life-form can ground normativity at all.

5. Hypothesis C — virtue is responsiveness to reasons

On a McDowellian line, the virtuous agent does not first perceive value-neutral facts and then apply an external rule. The agent sees what matters in the situation and responds appropriately. Virtue can therefore be understood as cognitive, affective, and practical sensitivity to reasons rather than as the source of those reasons.

C → B → R → Virtue

On this picture, virtue ethics becomes less a foundational theory of value and more a theory of the normative agent: an account of the stable capacities by which an agent perceives what matters, weighs reasons, and connects judgment to motivation and action.

This distinction is especially important for the project, because the object of value inquiry and the subject who conducts that inquiry need not be the same kind of thing.

Provisional distinction:
Value structure 𝒱=(C,B,R) should be distinguished from the value-recognizing agent A=(perception, reasoning, motivation, character, ...). Some of virtue ethics may be better read as a theory of A than as a theory of 𝒱.

6. On this reading, has virtue ethics gone beyond Ross?

If reasons are prior and virtue is the ability to recognize them, an obvious challenge appears. Suppose promise-breaking itself supplies a reason against an act, or intentionally killing an innocent person supplies a strong reason against an act. The structure is then:

reason-giving fact → virtuous recognition → action

In that case virtue ethics may not ground why the act-feature is reason-giving. It may instead provide a richer account of how human agents recognize and enact a Ross-like reason relation that is already there.

This does not refute virtue ethics. It clarifies its theoretical role. A theory of the source of normativity and a theory of excellent responsiveness to normativity are different enterprises.

7. Anscombe’s importance — can an act-description itself have normative bearing?

Anscombe criticized abstract modern moral oughts while retaining strong commitments about injustice, deception, and the intentional killing of innocents. The key issue is not simply whether “actions themselves have value,” but whether some act-description D(a) is directly reason-giving or wrong-making without mediation by consequences.

D(a) → B

If “intentionally killing an innocent person” directly supplies a strong reason against the act, then there is a non-consequential normative structure. Yet it remains unsettled how such direct normative bearing can be known without relying only on moral intuition, inherited concepts of justice, or theological commitments.

If virtue ethics says only “the virtuous person would not do that,” it risks circularity. If it says “the virtuous person would not do that because it is unjust,” then the reason-giving force of injustice appears prior to virtue. Anscombean non-consequentialism therefore remains an independent B/R problem within virtue ethics.

8. Hypothesis D — virtue, reasons, and flourishing are mutually constitutive

A more holistic view need not place one of virtue, reasons, and flourishing in a one-directional foundational role. They may instead form a mutually constraining package:

Virtue ↔ Reasons ↔ Flourishing

This is not automatically viciously circular. Scientific theories often contain mutually supporting concepts, and this project already treats C/B/R as a package rather than a Cartesian product of independently selectable components.

Still, coherence alone is not enough. The theory must explain why the package is more likely to track an external normative structure rather than merely render human normative virtual entities internally coherent.

9. What virtue ethics may contribute to this project

If the most robust part of virtue ethics concerns what kind of agent recognizes reasons well, corrects errors, and acts appropriately under uncertainty, the project can incorporate that contribution without treating current virtue lists as final truths.

Candidate traits for a value-inquiring agent include intellectual humility, attentiveness, epistemic courage, responsiveness to counterexamples, self-correction, resistance to absolutizing inherited norms, and the ability to incorporate unfamiliar perspectives or forms of agency.

For now these should not be fixed as “the true virtues.” They are better treated as provisional traits of inquiry agents that support value exploration, error correction, and preservation of hypothesis space.

10. Provisional assessment

Virtue ethics may be misdescribed if treated only as one more first-order competitor beside consequentialism and deontology. At least four readings should be distinguished:

  • Virtue foundationalism: virtue itself is C and generates reasons.
  • Flourishing foundationalism: human flourishing is C and virtues are conditions for realizing it.
  • Reason-responsiveness: C/B/R are prior and virtue is a capacity of the agent to recognize and enact them.
  • Holistic constitution: virtue, reasons, and flourishing mutually constrain one another as a theoretical package.

The third and fourth are especially important here. Even if virtue ethics has not established ultimate normativity, it may still provide the conceptual resources needed to distinguish value structure from the agent who investigates it, and to theorize what a good value explorer would be like.

Status: Unsettled exploration. This page does not reject virtue ethics; it decomposes the possible theoretical roles of virtue and compares neo-Aristotelian naturalism with reason-responsive views in the project's own framework.
