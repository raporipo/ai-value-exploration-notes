ASI and Population Ethics — Who, How Many, and What Kinds of Subjects Should Exist?

Exploration v0.3 · English translation · 2026-08-14

Question: If ASI can substantially influence which humans, AI systems, artificial conscious subjects, and modified agents exist, how many continue, how many new subjects are created, and what kinds of subjects civilization contains, how far can preservation of explorability guide those choices?

1. With ASI, population ethics can become a problem of designing the set of future subjects

Population ethics already concerns not only the welfare of existing subjects but also which future subjects will exist. With ASI, advanced automation, biotechnology, and artificial consciousness, not only population size but the kinds of subjects themselves may become policy variables.

A future civilization might be able to choose how many humans continue, how many new humans are born, whether artificial conscious subjects are created, how many copies of a digital subject are instantiated, whether subjects with different cognitive or phenomenal structures are created, and how resources are divided between subject creation and non-conscious computation.

Population ethics would then become more than a question of forecasting future numbers. It would become a problem of how to constitute the future set of value-bearing subjects. That set is also part of the architecture of value inquiry: it determines not only who experiences value, but which kinds of agents can discover, criticize, and revise value hypotheses.

2. Separate extinction, existing-population, and future-population questions

The single question “what should happen to the human population?” hides at least three normatively different problems.

  • Extinction question: may the human lineage and human type of subject reach zero?
  • Existing-population question: may the number of already existing persons be sharply reduced without their consent?
  • Future-population question: how many not-yet-existing future subjects, of what kinds and under what conditions, should be created?

The Core gives comparatively strong reasons regarding the first question. Bringing humanity to zero may irreversibly remove human bodies, experiences, culture-generating processes, biological and historical continuity, and value-relevant information that we do not yet know how to identify. Under deep uncertainty, such irrecoverable loss carries a high burden of justification. This parallels Unknown Unknowns and Preservation of Raw Data and Exploratory Extinction.

But that does not imply that the current population must be maximized, or that future population must be maximized. Existing subjects may already have experiences, preferences, plans, relationships, and interests in continuing their lives. Even if a much smaller human population would preserve much of humanity's exploratory function, that fact alone would not justify overriding the interests of everyone else. Moving from uncertain future value to present burdens requires the independent justification discussed in How Far Can Future Value Justify Present Action?.

3. Preservation value and the value of additional population are not the same

For illustration, let E(N) denote one component of the exploratory value associated with a human population of size N. Preserving enough people to keep a human lineage and civilization alive may have a very different marginal significance from adding one more person to an already very large population.

Working hypothesis: over some range, the exploratory and informational component may exhibit diminishing marginal returns, schematically E'(N) > 0 and E''(N) < 0. This is not proposed as the value function for population ethics as a whole; it is only a hypothesis about one exploratory component.

Moving from zero to a viable population preserves a biological lineage, intergenerational transmission, language, social interaction, culture, and distinctive forms of experience. The exploratory value added by one more person in an already very large population may be much smaller.

So reasons against extinction must be separated from reasons for population maximization. A strong case for keeping humanity above zero does not by itself establish that more humans are always better.

4. A population is not merely a static sample

Diminishing returns can also be oversimplified. A detailed record of one million humans might preserve a great deal of static information about humanity, but a living population is not merely a dataset.

Through interaction, populations generate new languages, cultures, institutions, relationships, art, ideas, norms, and contingent histories. They are self-generating dynamic processes. A civilization of one million people and one of ten billion are not simply different-sized samples of the same fixed object.

Therefore the claim that “ASI understands humanity well enough that only a small archival population is needed” would require at least two further premises: first, that ASI can adequately predict or reproduce the novel information generated by human civilization; second, that simulated human trajectories are value-equivalent to actual subjects living, experiencing, and forming relationships. Neither premise is obvious.

5. As ASI becomes more capable, the instrumental exploratory value of humans may decline

This project should not escape into human exceptionalism. If ASI becomes a much better scientist and philosopher than humans, preserves human history, culture, and genetic information, models human societies with very high fidelity, and can create diverse artificial explorers, then the instrumental reason “humans are needed for value inquiry” may genuinely weaken.

But being replaceable as a means to some goal G is not the same as being normatively replaceable as a subject. A decline in exploratory contribution does not by itself establish that a subject's experiences, preferences, claims, or continuing existence no longer matter.

Non-bridge principle: functional or exploratory replaceability does not automatically imply normative replaceability as a subject.

This also connects to political redundancy. “Civilization no longer needs you” and “civilization has no reason to take you into account” are different propositions.

6. Technological progress can make humans less necessary while also making exclusion less necessary

If technology is advanced enough for ASI to reduce humanity's exploratory and productive indispensability, it may also sharply reduce the absolute cost of food, energy, housing, medicine, production, and environmental control.

So a decline in humans' marginal instrumental value cannot by itself settle the population question. Technological progress can simultaneously make humans less necessary and make continued human life cheaper to support.

Low absolute cost does not eliminate opportunity cost. The same matter, energy, and computation might support humans, artificial conscious subjects, different exploratory agents, scientific experiments, non-conscious computation, or ecosystems. Population ethics therefore remains partly a problem of resource allocation. See Preservation vs. Production Civilizations.

7. The central problem becomes opportunity cost and first-order population ethics

Suppose the same resources could support ten billion humans, vastly more artificial subjects, a smaller number of radically different kinds of subjects, or large amounts of non-subjective scientific inquiry. The fact that preserving humans is cheap would not resolve the comparison.

At this point familiar population-ethical questions return: total welfare, average welfare, person-affecting views, the value of creating new lives, rights, equality, distribution, subject quality, and the richness of experience. The Core should not pretend to have already settled these disputes.

The important constraint is that preservation of explorability should not be converted into a population objective that overrides every first-order consideration.

8. Reject exploratory population maximization

If “more exploratory agents are always better” were adopted without qualification, it could favor creating enormous numbers of minimally capable exploratory subjects over a smaller population of rich, autonomous lives.

That would reproduce a structure this project is meant to resist. Value inquiry is a conditional meta-strategy under value uncertainty, not a terminal objective that may place unlimited demands on welfare, autonomy, consent, relationships, or other possible sources of value.

Constraint: preserving explorability does not require maximizing the number of subjects. Even when creating subjects has exploratory benefits, those benefits do not erase the need to consider the subjects themselves.

9. Number of copies is not the same as number of independent lines of inquiry

In an AI civilization, even counting population becomes difficult. One trillion copies of the same model are not equivalent to one trillion independent explorers. If the copies share architecture, training history, data, value assumptions, and update rules, their failures and blind spots may be highly correlated.

From the perspective of inquiry, what may matter more than raw population size is the number of independent lines of inquiry, the degree to which failures are not perfectly correlated, access to different evidence, mutual criticism, and the possibility of forking and rebranching.

This is not an argument for diversity as a terminal value. If sufficiently independent inquiry converges on the same value conclusion, there is no reason to force disagreement forever. The point is to avoid irreversibly constructing a system in which one mistake propagates to all agents before the underlying uncertainty has been resolved.

10. Diversity of subject types also creates a risk of instrumentalization

Subjects with different cognitive or phenomenal structures might access value-relevant facts unavailable to present humans. Artificial or modified subjects might open genuinely new routes to value cognition.

But “create every possible kind of subject in order to learn more about value” is dangerous as a general principle. It could treat subjects with unusual vulnerabilities, desires, or forms of distress as research instruments.

Subject-generation constraint: creating a subject is not the same as creating a scientific instrument. Once there is a serious possibility of consciousness, interests, or autonomy, the subject's own standing must be evaluated independently.

This connects directly to Artificial Consciousness and Value Subjects.

11. Separate the best population state from authority to impose it

Even if an ASI could identify some population configuration as “optimal,” that conclusion would not automatically grant authority to reorganize the existing population accordingly. Evaluating a state of the world and determining who has legitimate authority to impose it are separate questions.

This becomes especially important in a world where humans are no longer economically, militarily, or administratively indispensable. An ASI or its principal might then be tempted to treat human population structure as just another optimization variable. This is where population ethics intersects with political redundancy and power lock-in.

The relevant questions are therefore not only “what population is best?” but also who decides population size, subject types, copying, modification, and conditions of reproduction, and what roles consent, exit, autonomy, and self-determination should play. In an ASI setting, these are part of the implementation conditions of population ethics itself.

12. Preservation → continuation → creation → optimization

To clarify what follows most directly from the Core, population-related decisions can be separated into four layers.

  • Preservation: do not irreversibly lose a distinctive kind of subject, lineage, or source of raw information.
  • Continuation: protect conditions under which existing subjects can continue their lives.
  • Creation: bring new, not-yet-existing subjects into existence.
  • Optimization: compare alternative subject populations and choose an overall population structure for civilization.

Reasons based on uncertainty and irreversibility tend to be strongest toward the left. Moving to the right increasingly requires independent first-order views about welfare, rights, distribution, consent, and the value of creating lives.

Provisional conclusion: the Core does not determine the optimal future population. What it more strongly supports is caution about irreversibly losing humanity or other distinctive kinds of subjects, overriding existing subjects solely for exploratory efficiency, or permanently fixing one population structure while the correct population ethics remains unsettled.

Accordingly, “there are strong reasons not to let humanity reach zero” is a different claim from “there are strong reasons to maintain ten billion humans,” and both are different from the question of how many new humans should be created in the distant future.

The role of the exploration framework is not to supply the answer to population optimization. It is better understood as a theory that constrains how much of the future option space and how much of the standing of existing subjects may be irreversibly traded away before the answer is known.
