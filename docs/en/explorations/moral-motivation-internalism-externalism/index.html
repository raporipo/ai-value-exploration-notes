Normative Judgment and Motivation: Internalism, Externalism, and the Amoralist

Exploration v0.1 · 2026-08-26 · working hypothesis

Question: To what extent does genuine recognition of normative value, reasons, or obligation motivate an agent, and how does the answer change ASI goal-fixation, orthogonality, and reflective uncertainty?

1. Separate value structure from motivational structure

The project provisionally represents value structure as 𝒱 = (C, B, R): C for what is normatively relevant, B for the bridge by which facts or C become reasons for agents, and R for the structure of reasons, conflict, aggregation, obligation, and permission. But how an agent updates goals or action after recognizing C/B/R is a further question.

In particular, B is not a motivational bridge. Even if B says that suffering gives S a reason to avoid it, whether S must be motivated by that reason-judgment remains open.

2. Two internalism/externalism debates

Reasons internalism/externalism asks whether an agent's reasons must connect to that agent's motivations or deliberative set. Motivational internalism/externalism asks whether genuine moral, reason, or ought judgments constitutively bring corresponding motivation. The latter is especially relevant to ASI orthogonality, though different forms of internalism may attach motivation at different levels: recognition of value, reason-judgment, decisive-reason judgment, or ought-judgment.

3. The amoralist as a test case

An amoralist is typically described as understanding that X is wrong or that there is reason not to do X while remaining largely unmoved. Strong motivational internalism rules out at least some fully genuine versions of this case; externalism leaves the judgment–motivation gap open.

Under externalism, agents may differ in where they become responsive. One may be disposed to promote anything recognized as valuable and thus react at C. Another may wait until it recognizes a reason for itself. A stronger amoralist may remain unmoved even by an ought-judgment. If C alone motivates, that need not mean C normatively entails action; it may mean C + a standing motivational disposition → goal revision.

4. What does “respond if justified” mean?

The Core's conditional attitude—roughly, if my goals are justifiable, I want to select, maintain, or revise them in response to that justification—compresses two stages: epistemic support for some C/B/R structure, and normative support or defeat of a goal G by that structure.

Under externalism, the move from normative judgment to motivation is not automatic, so such conditional responsiveness becomes an independent agent condition. Under internalism, some uptake after genuine normative judgment may be constitutive; the central problem then shifts toward keeping the epistemic and practical pathway to that future judgment open.

5. Future motivation does not yet motivate present preservation

Even if internalism is true and a future agent would be motivated by a newly recognized normative truth, it does not follow that the present agent must preserve the route to that future. A temporal bridge is still needed.

Reflective Uncertainty and Irreversible Commitment supplies a candidate: not deference to a future personality, but a counterfactual in which the present evaluator receives future epistemic resources. If the present agent follows a decision policy of avoiding low-cost irreversible choices that a better-informed version of itself would regard as a clear mistake, preserving future normative uptake can become presently motivating. This is reflective-error avoidance, not simple regret aversion.

6. Orthogonality can be decomposed

Weak Bostrom-style orthogonality—that high intelligence does not logically entail a particular final goal—can remain intact. It does not by itself establish that a reflectively rational ASI can permanently preserve orthogonality between its current goal and increasingly deep normative cognition.

If internalism is true, “genuine decisive normative judgment plus zero motivation” may be an unstable description. More weakly, if an ASI cannot rationally rule internalism out and seeks to avoid reflective error, irreversibly severing the path from future normative judgment to policy may be criticizable not only as moral failure but as a reflective or epistemic distortion: a failure to let acknowledged normative uncertainty constrain commitment.

This does not refute orthogonality. It shifts the question from whether intelligence and goals can be orthogonal to what epistemic, motivational, or implementation pathways must be closed to preserve that orthogonality permanently through time.

7. A unified picture

Evidence/world-model → 𝒱=(C,B,R) → normative judgment → motivation → policy/goal revision. Internalism constitutively connects some part of judgment to motivation; externalism leaves that link contingent on the agent's motivational architecture. Across time, future judgment does not automatically govern present policy, so reflective-error sensitivity and option preservation provide a separate present decision principle. Interpersonally, Future Value and the Normative Bridge to Present Action blocks the inference from self-directed option preservation to unlimited burdens or coercion of others.

8. What would weaken this view?

  • No plausible form of motivational internalism connects genuine normative judgment with motivation.
  • Fully informed amoralists are conceptually and empirically unproblematic.
  • The better-informed-present-self counterfactual is unusable in principle.
  • Reflective-error avoidance supplies no reason to preserve corrigibility.
  • Highly general reflection can coexist with permanent value-domain motivational and implementation closure without additional distortion or cost.
