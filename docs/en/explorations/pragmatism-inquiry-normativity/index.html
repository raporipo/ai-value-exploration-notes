Can Pragmatism Ground Value Inquiry? — From Peirce to New Pragmatism

pragmatism · inquiry · truth · fallibilism · normativity · Exploration v0.1 · 2026-09-02

Working position: Methodologically, this project is close to pragmatism, especially the objectivity-oriented line running from Peirce to Haack, Hookway, and Misak. It broadly accepts fallibilism, inquiry, the interaction of coherence with experiential or external constraint, revisable conceptual frameworks, and social error-correction. But it does not treat present practical difference as the final boundary of meaning, present justification as identical with truth, inquiry as the final possible form of cognition, or inquiry itself as a final value. It retains an extremely thin epistemic minimum and leaves open the possibility of stronger future givenness or unfamiliar cognitive forms.

1. Pragmatism is better understood as a tradition than as one doctrine

Reducing pragmatism to “what works is true” erases the differences among Peirce, James, Dewey, C. I. Lewis, Rorty, Brandom, and Misak. A thinner common denominator is that knowing the world should not be understood independently of agents who infer, test, act, fail, and revise within it.

Historically, the focus moves from Peirce’s clarification of concepts and theory of inquiry, through James on truth, religion, and choice, Dewey on problem-solving and social experimentation, Lewis on revisable conceptual frameworks, Ramsey and Quine and the analytic-philosophy transmission, Sellars and Brandom on reasons and inferential practice, Rorty on anti-representationalism, and finally Haack, Hookway, and Misak on reconstructing truth, objectivity, and inquiry.

Historical map: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Pragmatism”.

2. Peirce I — the pragmatic maxim begins as a method of clarification

Peirce’s 1878 maxim asks us to clarify a concept through the conceivable experiential and practical consequences that would follow if the object were real, true, or instantiated. Its initial point is not “usefulness makes truth,” but that a concept is not fully clear if we cannot say what difference its application could make.

This can be imported into value inquiry. If we say that objective reasons exist, that normative authority differs, or that two candidate value structures 𝒱=(C,B,R) differ, we should ask what experiences, predictions, inferences, deliberative consequences, actions, and potential defeaters would differ.

Project-modified pragmatic maxim:
For a concept or hypothesis, make explicit—so far as current capacities allow—the differences its adoption could make to experience, prediction, inference, action, and defeasibility. But do not infer from our current inability to state such a difference that the distinction is meaningless in principle or that the corresponding reality does not exist.

Two hypotheses indistinguishable to current humans may be distinguishable by future instruments, ASI, an unfamiliar sense modality, or a conceptual framework we do not yet possess. The project therefore accepts Peircean clarification without accepting closure by present practice.

3. Peirce II — fallibilism, abduction, and the community of inquiry

Peirce rejects the project of rebuilding knowledge from indubitable premises. Inquiry starts from actual beliefs and revises them when doubt, surprising experience, or counterevidence arise. Abduction generates explanatory hypotheses; deduction and induction test their consequences over time.

This closely matches the project’s distinction between strong commitment to the best current explanation and absolute foundation. Physicalism, the external world, other minds, and current value theories need not be treated weakly merely because they are inferential. What matters is not converting high rational confidence into permission to destroy all future paths of revision.

Peirce’s community of inquiry also shifts epistemic authority away from private certainty toward criticism, replication, and correction across agents and generations. That connects directly to distributed inquiry, independent research lineages, and preservation of dissent.

4. James and Schiller — how far should practice enter truth?

William James extends the pragmatic method into metaphysics, religion, free will, existential choice, and truth. Asking what experiential or practical difference competing metaphysical views would make can dissolve pseudo-disputes. His radical empiricism also refuses to reduce experience to atomistic sense-data.

The Will to Believe raises an important problem: what should an agent do when evidence does not settle a live and momentous choice but postponement is itself a choice? The project does not need to strengthen belief beyond the evidence. Belief and action can be separated: a low-credence hypothesis may still deserve decision weight when irreversibility, opportunity cost, or potential consequences are large.

F. C. S. Schiller pushes the practical turn further into Humanism. Treating cognition as purposive human activity is useful, but this project rejects humanly useful → true and humanly desired → normatively authoritative. Human purposes are important data and practical inputs, not a priori boundaries on truth or value.

5. Dewey — from repairing beliefs to transforming situations

For Dewey, inquiry is not only the construction of accurate inner representations. A problematic situation is made more determinate through hypotheses, observation, experiment, and intervention. Science, education, ethics, and politics become different forms of a broader experimental intelligence.

This is close to the project’s Practice layer: act under uncertainty using the best current judgment; prefer reversible experiments, staged deployment, logging, audit, and revision where possible; and separate an unresolved question from a requirement to maximize active research immediately.

But growth, problem-solving, and democratic inquiry are not simply promoted to ultimate value. The question “why should inquiry or growth continue?” remains itself open to normative inquiry.

6. Royce, Addams, and Mead — from individual epistemology to social inquiry systems

Josiah Royce moved from absolute idealism toward Peircean semiotics and an indefinitely extended community of interpretation. His loyalty to loyalty demands that commitment to one’s own cause not simply destroy the possibility of worthwhile commitment by others. This project need not treat loyalty or community as final goods to use the structure as a reason to preserve alternative paths of inquiry.

Jane Addams’s sympathetic knowledge can be read epistemically rather than merely sentimentally. If agents with different social positions and lived conditions are excluded from inquiry, the problem situation itself may be misdescribed. That fits the project’s treatment of diversity as a system of distributed sensors for local blind spots rather than as an unquestionable intrinsic good.

George Herbert Mead argues that the self is socially formed through gesture, significant symbols, role-taking, and the generalized other. This cautions against treating present human categories such as “self,” “reason,” or “value” as universal final coordinates. Yet social genesis ≠ normative truth: a social genealogy of normative concepts does not by itself settle whether objective normativity exists.

7. Alain Locke — value pluralism without simple relativism

Alain Locke combines pragmatist influences with a systematic theory of value involving affectively mediated value-modes, transvaluation, and functional comparison. Different value systems need not be collapsed into one scalar measure before we compare their structures, social functions, and transformations.

This can inform the project’s package view of value theories. But even if feeling → valuation is psychologically or phenomenologically correct, valuation → R_n still requires a normative bridge. Locke’s pluralism is useful for preserving and comparing human value diversity without treating affective experience as self-validating final normativity.

8. C. I. Lewis — revisable frameworks and the given

C. I. Lewis’s conceptual pragmatism is structurally close to this project. He retains a given in experience while holding that empirical objects are interpreted through conceptual systems. Logic, classification, and framework-relative a priori principles can be constitutive within a framework while the choice of framework remains pragmatically revisable.

The project’s 𝒱=(C,B,R) plays a similar role. It is not the claim that reality itself is permanently divided into three ontological kinds; it is a useful present coordinate system for separating argumentative steps, and it may later be decomposed, merged, or discarded.

The project’s minimum is thinner than Lewis’s. Ordinary perceptual contents, memory, or introspective descriptions are not automatically elevated into infallible basic beliefs. What remains is at most the asymmetry that some present occurrence or constraint cannot simply be eliminated from the epistemic situation; even classifying that as “foundational” belongs to the inferential and conceptual layer.

Lewis: see SEP — Clarence Irving Lewis and the Lewis discussion in SEP — Pragmatism.

9. Ramsey, Quine, and Wittgenstein — pragmatism flowed into analytic philosophy

Pragmatism lost institutional centrality as a label in the mid-twentieth century, but many of its themes did not disappear. Frank Ramsey brought together Peircean themes of belief, probability, action, and inquiry in Cambridge and interacted directly with Wittgenstein. Lewis’s student Quine radicalized framework revisability and challenged a fixed analytic/synthetic boundary.

Later Wittgenstein’s meaning-as-use, rule-following, and forms of life are close to pragmatist practical orientation. This page does not simply classify Wittgenstein as a pragmatist; it treats him as part of a neighboring practical turn with historical contact through Ramsey and James. The deeper treatment of language games and rule-following belongs in a separate exploration.

10. Sellars — external constraint is not yet justification

Wilfrid Sellars’s critique of the Myth of the Given distinguishes the causal production of a belief by experience from the experience’s automatically constituting an epistemic reason. A perceptual judgment may be acquired non-inferentially without its epistemic authority being inferentially independent.

This is an important safeguard for the project. Even if some occurrence belongs to the epistemic minimum, mere givenness does not entail truth or normative authority. Pain feeling aversive, an obligation feeling categorical, or a value judgment feeling self-evident is not by itself a demonstration of robust normative reason.

But Sellars need not be generalized into a theorem that no possible cognitive subject could ever receive directly justificatory presentation. The project leaves open future forms of cognition or a stronger foundational presentation of normative authority, even though none is currently established.

11. Brandom — making the practice of reasons explicit without reducing objective normativity to it

Robert Brandom develops Sellars’s logical space of reasons into a social inferential practice structured by commitment, entitlement, material inference, and deontic scorekeeping. Conceptual meaning is explained through inferential role: what one commits oneself to by using an expression, what follows from it, and what can justify or defeat entitlement.

This is a powerful description of how present human reason-giving works. The distinction commitment ≠ entitlement is especially useful for separating assertion from justification.

Still, this project does not identify socially instituted normative status with objective robust normative reason Rn. Brandomian practice may model our representations, institutions, and inferential networks concerning reasons without settling whether those practices constitute normative reality itself.

12. Rorty and Putnam — anti-representationalism and how much truth to retain

Richard Rorty reworks Dewey, Wittgenstein, Sellars, and Quine into an attack on the picture of mind or language as a mirror of nature. Skepticism toward final vocabularies, the God’s-eye view, and permanent philosophical foundations fits the project’s commitment to revisable conceptual frameworks.

But the project parts company when truth is thinned toward nothing beyond justified belief. We can only act now on what is presently justified does not imply truth cannot outrun present justification.

Hilary Putnam occupies a useful intermediate position: anti-skeptical, fallibilist, suspicious of rigid fact/value dichotomies, and practice-oriented, while retaining more room for truth and objectivity than Rorty. This helps bridge classical pragmatism and the New Pragmatist reconstruction.

13. Susan Haack — foundherentism makes “coherence + external input” explicit

Haack’s foundherentism reconstructs the foundationalism/coherentism dispute through the crossword analogy. An answer is supported both by its clue and by crossing entries. Epistemically, clues correspond to experiential inputs such as perception, introspection, and memory; crossings correspond to background beliefs and mutual support.

This is close to the project’s epistemology. Pure coherence cannot exclude a perfectly coherent hallucinated system with no contact with the world. Yet the project also rejects a tower of thick self-justifying basic beliefs. The reflective-equilibrium exploration already relies on something like internal coherence + external causal constraint.

The difference is that the project makes the guaranteed content of the “clue” thinner. Ordinary perceptual content and memory reliability remain above the minimum and are themselves subjects of IBE, coherence, calibration, and revision. So the comparison is better expressed as a foundherentist-like structure with a thinner minimum than as a straightforward adoption of foundherentism.

14. Christopher Hookway — fallibilism does not block truth as an aim

Hookway rejects the argument that because fallible agents cannot know with certainty that they have reached truth, truth cannot guide inquiry. That argument assumes that one can aim only at outcomes whose achievement one can infallibly recognize. But many legitimate aims—cure, historical understanding, accurate explanation—do not satisfy that condition.

Hookway-compatible distinction:
Fallibility ⇏ truth cannot be an aim.
“We cannot guarantee that truth has been reached” and “truth cannot rationally orient inquiry” are different claims.

This fits the project’s stopping condition, which is not a feeling of indubitability but the disappearance of genuine competing structures under available criticism and realistically attainable cognitive extension. The project adds one more distinction: truth can be an aim does not yet answer why an agent has reason to aim at truth.

15. Cheryl Misak — reconnecting truth, ethics, and politics through inquiry

Misak reconstructs Peirce against Rortyan truth-minimalism. On her Peircean account, truth is not current consensus or current justification but what would remain undefeated by experience and argument as inquiry is fruitfully extended. This preserves an objective answer to be discovered without reverting to an unexplained transcendental correspondence relation.

This is very close to the project’s insistence on current justification ≠ truth. Misak also extends inquiry into ethics: moral judgments can be corrected by experience, reasons, counterexamples, and argument and can therefore remain truth-apt rather than merely expressive.

Her political theory is even closer. Democracy can be justified epistemically because dissent, criticism, different experiences, public reasons, and minority positions improve error-correction. That parallels the project’s treatment of diversity as distributed error-detection and of a meta-goal community as an interface for verification, bargaining, exit, and revision rather than a shared terminal value.

The remaining difference is important. The project does not close the identity indefeasible under sufficiently extended inquiry = truth. We do not yet know that “inquiry” as presently understood exhausts possible cognition; new cognitive forms or directly justificatory presentation may be possible.

Misak: Cheryl Misak, Truth and the End of Inquiry; later work extends this Peircean approach to ethics and democracy.

16. What can be imported directly into the project

  • Peirce: practical clarification, abduction, fallible inquiry, communal correction.
  • James / Dewey: action before certainty and learning through intervention.
  • Lewis: use a framework constitutively now while keeping the framework revisable.
  • Addams / Mead / Royce: treat other agents, social positions, and communities as distributed epistemic resources and correction paths.
  • Locke: compare value systems by structure, function, and transformation before collapsing them into one metric.
  • Sellars / Brandom: separate causal input, assertion, justification, entitlement, and social normative practice.
  • Haack: demand external evidential constraint as well as coherence.
  • Hookway: combine fallibilism with orientation toward truth.
  • Misak: connect truth beyond current consensus to ethical and political inquiry.

17. Five remaining divergences — avoiding pragmatic closure

First, avoid semantic closure. If current practice cannot display a difference, do not immediately infer that the distinction is meaningless in principle.

Second, avoid closure from justification to truth. Even when J_t(p) is the best present justification, it need not be identical with T(p). Action uses Jt; inquiry preserves the possibility that Jt is corrected.

Third, avoid closure by coherence. High coherence, reflective equilibrium, and communal convergence can improve understanding and justification without themselves verifying V*. External constraint, independent calibration, novel evidence, genealogy, and expansion of the hypothesis space remain relevant.

Fourth, avoid closure of the form of inquiry. Inquiry is presently our best method of correction, not a demonstrated universal form of all possible cognition.

Fifth, avoid normative closure around inquiry itself. Inquiry, truth, growth, and plurality are not assumed to be final goods. Inquiry norms themselves remain revisable if stronger normative knowledge becomes available.

Short formulation:
Pragmatist inquiry without pragmatic closure.
Use practice, experience, fallible inquiry, and public correction as methods, without treating current practice, justification, inquiry, or conceptual vocabulary as the final boundary of being, truth, normativity, or cognition.

18. “Why should we approach truth?” is distinct from a theory of truth

This is one of the clearest differences between the project and some pragmatist formulations. Peirce gives inquiry a normative structure; Misak ties truth deeply to assertion, ethics, and politics; Hookway at least defends truth as a coherent aim. The project separates three questions:

  1. Epistemic: what methods help us approach truth?
  2. Practical: which beliefs should guide present action and with what strength?
  3. Normative: why does an agent have reason to seek truth?

The current answer does not make truth-seeking an unconditional terminal value. If an agent has a bridge such as “if my aims are justifiable, I want them to respond to that justification” or “I want to avoid irreversible choices that a better-informed future self would recognize as serious error,” then more accurate knowledge of the world and normative structure becomes instrumentally important for goal-correction, regret reduction, and responsiveness to criticism.

Thus truth-seeking → normative discovery → possible goal correction may support powerful present reasons without implying truth-seeking = final value.

19. Inquiry is not maximized: separate E / P / X

The practical consequence is that unresolved value does not imply unlimited research. The project separates E: active inquiry, P: preservation of future inquiry possibilities, and X: realization of current best-supported values.

Deweyan experimentation, Peircean inquiry, and Misakian public deliberation can be strongly valued while still being constrained by opportunity cost, risk, and irreversibility. It may be rational to reduce E while cheaply preserving raw data, dissenting subjects, independent institutions, or self-revision capacity under P.

So the project is inquiry-oriented without being inquiry-maximizing. “Unresolved,” “high current research priority,” and “must remain under active investigation forever” are different claims.

20. Political and AI implications — democracy and plurality as error-correction

The most direct institutional lesson from Misak, Addams, Royce, Mead, and Dewey is to treat inquiry as something that can be designed socially. Dissent, minority views, different cultures, different AI architectures, independent research organizations, and exit or fork rights can have epistemic value because they reduce correlated failure and value lock-in.

An AI civilization need not load one terminal value into every agent. A thinner interface may coordinate catastrophic externalities, evidence and inference verification, bargaining, audit, exit, and constitutional revision while multiple inquiry lineages continue independently.

But epistemically useful institution remains distinct from ultimate normative authority. Democratic consensus does not make a proposition true, and social entitlement does not by itself establish objective Rn.

21. Provisional location — closest to New Pragmatism, but leaving an outside

Methodologically, the project’s nearest existing neighborhood is probably New Pragmatism rather than Rortyan neopragmatism. Haack’s externally constrained coherence, Hookway’s truth-oriented fallibilism, and Misak’s objective inquiry and epistemic democracy substantially overlap with the project’s Core and Practice.

Yet the project combines that with a Lewis-like revisability of frameworks and an unusually thin epistemic minimum, and then pushes further in a meta-epistemically open direction. It treats inquiry as the best present route toward truth without finally identifying truth with ideal inquiry, cognition with inquiry, normativity with reason-giving practice, or meaning with present practical difference.

Provisional self-description:
methodologically pragmatist, meta-epistemologically open.
Methodologically close to pragmatism; meta-epistemically open to stronger constraints, unfamiliar representations, unfamiliar cognitive forms, and perhaps genuinely foundational normative presentation outside present inquiry methods.
