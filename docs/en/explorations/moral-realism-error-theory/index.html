Moral Realism, Error Theory, and Value-Like Appearance

After evolutionary debunking, the Darwinian dilemma, disagreement, and queerness, is value inquiry still open?

Exploration v0.4 · English translation · 2026-08-28

Working position: We should be deeply skeptical that present human moral judgments reliably track objective value. But that epistemic defeat does not by itself entail that objective value is unreal, or that it is unknowable in principle by every possible agent. This page accepts much of the pressure from Mackie, Joyce, and Street while refusing to lock in error theory itself, leaving open future foundational presentation, inferential discovery, and theoretical revision.

1. The question: are we entitled to believe in objective value?

The issue is not simply whether we have strong moral intuitions. At least four questions should be separated: whether stance-independent normative facts exist; how they could be known; whether present human moral judgments track them; and whether recognizing them must motivate an agent. This page focuses mainly on the epistemic and tracking questions.

2. Mackie: relativity and queerness

J. L. Mackie's error theory reads ordinary moral judgments as purporting to state objective, stance-independent normative truths, then argues that no such values exist. His argument from relativity emphasizes persistent moral variation across cultures and eras: different ways of life, institutions, interests, and traditions may explain divergent moral codes more economically than differential access to a shared moral reality.

His argument from queerness goes further. Objective values would apparently be not merely descriptive features of the world but intrinsically action-guiding or reason-giving. Such properties look ontologically unusual, and our alleged capacity to know them looks epistemologically unusual as well.

3. Joyce and evolutionary debunking

Evolutionary debunking arguments shift attention from the content of moral beliefs to their genealogy. Human tendencies toward cooperation, reciprocity, kin concern, harm aversion, and hostility to betrayal may be explicable by selection for survival and social coordination rather than by selection for tracking independent moral truths.

Debunking ≠ falsification: showing that the process that produced a belief in p is not truth-tracking does not itself establish ¬p. The immediate target is our epistemic entitlement to the belief.

This matters for the project: the stronger the debunking story, the weaker the case for treating present human values as already-discovered cosmic values. Yet the same argument does not directly establish that there are no normative truths.

4. Street's Darwinian dilemma: accepting the possibility of being off track

Sharon Street asks realist theories to explain the relation between evolutionarily shaped evaluative tendencies and stance-independent evaluative truths. If there is no tracking relation, the agreement between our judgments and truth looks accidental; if there is a tracking relation, realism owes a non-circular explanation of it.

Our response: do not assume that present human evaluative judgment must be substantially on track. We can accept the epistemic cost that humanity may be deeply value-ignorant rather than rescuing a strong truth-tracking story merely to preserve confidence in current morality.

But taking this horn does not mean that every moral belief is false, nor that normative knowledge is impossible for every possible agent. It means that we currently lack a secure guarantee that evolved human evaluation strongly tracks independent normative truth.

5. Accepting skepticism without locking in skepticism

Four claims must be distinguished: skepticism about particular moral beliefs; skepticism about the reliability of human moral cognition; skepticism about present humanity's ability to know objective normativity; and the much stronger claim that objective normativity is unknowable in principle by any sufficiently capable agent.

Evolutionary debunking can exert substantial pressure on the first three. The fourth requires an additional argument. A mismatch between our present cognitive equipment and its target is not the same as principled unknowability.

  • Real and knowable: objective value exists and sufficiently capable agents can know it.
  • Real but inaccessible: objective value exists but is in principle or in practice unavailable to finite agents.
  • Unreal: there are no stance-independent normative facts.

6. Reply to the argument from relativity

Persistent disagreement is evidence against confident claims of reliable tracking, but disagreement does not logically imply the absence of truth. Deep disagreement also occurs in philosophy, politics, religion, and sometimes in scientific theory choice without automatically implying that there is no fact of the matter.

This reply weakens the inference from moral variation to error theory while preserving variation as negative evidence about our epistemic reliability.

7. Companions in guilt: is morality uniquely queer?

A broader reply to queerness appeals to other kinds of normativity. If stance-independent reasons for action are too queer to exist, what about epistemic reasons? “Given strong evidence for H, one ought to believe H” also appears to be more than a report of psychological tendency.

This does not refute error theory. A skeptic can extend error theory to epistemic normativity. But then the issue is no longer that morality alone is suspicious: it becomes the general problem of whether objective normativity of any kind can be understood. This connects directly to Has Categorical Normativity Been Discovered?

8. Shafer-Landau: self-evidence and initial justification

Russ Shafer-Landau defends non-naturalist moral realism and allows that some basic moral propositions can have non-inferential or self-evident justification. Evolutionary influence need not contaminate every moral belief equally; sufficiently reflective judgments may provide a foothold from which other judgments can be criticized.

The project leaves this route open but asks a further question: how independent is the seeming self-evidence of a judgment from evolution, culture, learning, and cognitive architecture? Self-evidence therefore remains evidence, but not by itself a stopping condition.

9. Enoch: robust realism and third-factor explanations

David Enoch defends robust metanormative realism and confronts the evolutionary epistemic challenge directly. Third-factor strategies aim to explain the correlation between normative truth and evolved belief without positing a mysterious faculty of moral perception. If survival-promoting tendencies were selected and survival is independently normatively significant, some correlation can emerge.

The remaining worry is circularity: why are we entitled to assume at the relevant stage that survival is objectively valuable? If the very tendency to value survival is part of the evolutionary story under suspicion, a third-factor account must avoid simply building the disputed evaluative premise into its explanation.

10. Parfit: lightweight ontology, irreducible normativity

Derek Parfit rejects the picture of normative truths as strange normative particles inside the causal universe. Irreducibly normative truths may be more like logical, mathematical, or modal truths: their truth need not require an additional heavyweight entity.

This substantially reduces one version of Mackie's ontological queerness. But lighter ontology does not by itself solve epistemology. Even if irreducible normative truths can be true without queer causal objects, why trust present human rational intuition as a route to them? Parfit is more optimistic about rational reflection than this project is prepared to be.

11. What remains of queerness

The realist replies above do not eliminate Mackie's challenge. At present we do not possess a settled account of what objective normativity is, how it relates to physics, consciousness, reasons, or rationality, or how agents could know it.

Reply to Mackie: treat queerness as a serious current evidential cost, not as a permanent ontological exclusion rule. Error theory may be a strong present hypothesis without becoming an unrevisable final theory.

12. Three possible realist routes

Foundational presentation: normative force is presented as reason-giving in a way that can be distinguished from mere desire, conditioning, or evolved response. If such presentation had unusually strong epistemic credentials, it could approach a stopping condition for value inquiry.

Inferential discovery: objective value becomes the best explanation when phenomenal value, symmetry across agents, reasons, rationality, consciousness, and world structure are considered together. This could justify very high practical confidence while remaining revisable.

Structural identity or reduction: what currently looks like a distinct normative domain could turn out to be identical with a deeper natural, logical, or structural fact.

13. Three layers of value-like appearance

P: Phenomenal valence
Pain appears bad; pleasure appears attractive.
N: Agent-relative normativity
The appearance connects to a reason for this agent to avoid or pursue something.
U: Stance-independent normativity
The reason or value holds beyond the contingent desires and attitudes of the agent, potentially across agents.

Neither P→N nor N→U is automatic. Phenomenal valence can be important evidence without itself establishing universal normativity. Likewise, the appearance of categorical rational constraint must be distinguished from the objective validity of its content.

14. If error theory is true, inquiry still remains

If Mackie- or Joyce-style error theory is ultimately correct, value inquiry is no longer the discovery of a hidden true morality. But inquiry remains into actual evaluative architectures, pleasure and suffering, preference formation, institutions, cooperation, value modification, artificial agents, and the design of reflective value construction in a world without stance-independent answers.

If realism is true, by contrast, inquiry should search beyond current human intuitions for foundational presentation, inferential discovery, and possible convergence among differently constituted cognitive agents.

15. Practical paralysis does not automatically follow

Weak justification for current morality does not entail that no action is possible or that all choices are equivalent. Present desires, suffering, institutions, and cooperative norms remain actual inputs to decision-making even if they are not proven cosmic truths.

But reversibility and inquiry should not be smuggled in as newly discovered objective values. See Reflective Uncertainty and Irreversible Commitment.

16. Position of this project

  • Take evolutionary debunking seriously as pressure against confidence in current human moral cognition.
  • Accept that current evaluation could be substantially off track.
  • Retain Mackie's relativity and queerness arguments as serious debts for realism.
  • Treat Shafer-Landau, Enoch, and Parfit as strong realist replies without treating them as settled vindications of current human intuition.
  • Separate debunking from non-existence, and non-existence from principled unknowability.
  • Keep open future foundational presentation, inferential discovery, and structural identity.
Provisional conclusion: humanity may not be a civilization that has mostly discovered value; we may inhabit a value-epistemic desert. But ignorance does not yet entitle us to conclude that there is nothing there. The appropriate stance is neither faith in current value nor faith in error theory, but inquiry that remains revisable under future evidence.

17. Open questions

  • Can evolutionarily shaped reasoning reliably criticize evolutionarily shaped value without vicious self-support?
  • If error theory extends to epistemic normativity, how should arguments about what we ought to believe be understood?
  • How could foundational normative presentation be distinguished from extremely strong but mistaken certainty?
  • Which metaethical framework best explains the bridges P→N→U?
  • Would normative convergence among humans, AIs, and alien cognitive architectures be evidence of truth-tracking or merely convergence under shared constraints?
