Has Categorical Normativity Been Discovered?

Exploration v0.2 · English translation · 2026-08-28

Question: Has humanity already discovered normativity that gives reasons in itself, without presupposing a desire, end, or self-conception? And even if such a principle exists, have present humans correctly identified its universal scope and concrete application?

1. Kant's question is close to this project

Kant's categorical imperative differs from a hypothetical imperative of the form “if you want X, do Y.” It aims at a principle valid for rational agency independently of any particular desire or end. The project is therefore unusually close to this site's search for normative force that is not merely conditional on a preference.

It would be too weak to criticize Kant as though he were simply offering an ethic for people who happen to value rationality. Kant's stronger claim is that being a free will, determining oneself through practical reason, and autonomously legislating universal law are different expressions of the same underlying structure.

2. Reconstructing Kant in his strongest form

This page therefore reconstructs the Kantian route without first reducing it to a conditional preference:

will → practical reason → freedom → autonomy → self-legislation → universal law → categorical authority

The relevant claim is not “if you want to remain rational, universalize your maxims.” On the Kantian picture, a free will that is not heteronomously determined by external causes or contingent desires must determine itself through reason, and thus must be autonomous. An autonomous law, in turn, cannot be merely private preference; it must have a form that can hold for rational willing as such.

The real questions are therefore whether the transitions from freedom to autonomy, from autonomy to universal self-legislation, and from universal self-legislation to moral authority are in fact necessary.

3. From freedom to autonomy, and from autonomy to normativity

The first problem concerns the movement from negative freedom to positive autonomy. “Not determined from outside” is not obviously identical to “legislating a law for oneself.” Nor is self-legislation obviously identical to the legislation of a law valid for every rational agent.

The second problem is the distinction between constitutive necessity and normative necessity. Suppose rational agency constitutively requires conformity to some rule R. It does not immediately follow that there is an objective categorical reason to conform to R. That an activity cannot count as that activity without R and that one unconditionally ought to follow R are different claims.

This is where modern shmagency-style objections to Kantian constructivism and constitutivism become relevant. The point should not be oversimplified into “why want to be an agent?” The sharper question is whether being a constitutive condition of agency automatically generates a categorical reason to satisfy that condition.

4. From the Groundwork's deduction to the fact of reason

Kant's position is stronger than a one-way deduction from freedom to morality. In the Critique of Practical Reason, the consciousness that the moral law binds us comes to the foreground as the “fact of reason.” Here the moral law is not merely derived from freedom; our consciousness of moral law also provides practical certainty of freedom.

This matters for this project. If a reason were disclosed as binding in itself—not because “I want this” or “I chose that end”—then it would be epistemically stronger than a merely inferential ethical theory and could become a serious candidate for the conditions for ending value inquiry.

5. Does the fact of reason satisfy a stopping condition?

Two claims must nevertheless be separated:

First, that normative bindingness is presented in experience or practical consciousness.
Second, that the content presented is objectively valid.

A subject may encounter “I absolutely ought to do this” as a powerful epistemic appearance. Yet it remains a further question whether this is the self-manifestation of pure practical reason or a normative disposition shaped by evolution, culture, learning, and cognitive architecture. The availability of such causal explanations does not by itself refute Kant, but it creates a discrimination problem: why identify this appearance as pure reason rather than as an empirically produced moral experience?

This page therefore does not dismiss the fact of reason. It treats it as one of the strongest existing candidates for a basic normative appearance while denying that the appearance and its objective validity may simply be identified without further argument.

6. Separate the categorical imperative from its application

At least three levels must be distinguished:

the supreme principle itself ≠ general duties derived from it ≠ concrete applications in human social life

Kant himself allows that applying a pure moral principle to finite human beings requires empirical knowledge of human nature and circumstances, as well as judgment. The truth of Kant's supreme principle is therefore distinct from the universal correctness of every concrete duty or example Kant himself offers.

The epistemic question is not merely whether we know the universal principle. It is also whether we can formulate the relevant maxim correctly, model its universalization correctly, identify the relevant contradiction correctly, and move from the formal principle to a concrete duty without importing unnoticed empirical assumptions.

7. What do the four examples actually show?

False promising

Of the four examples, false promising is comparatively strong. A maxim that exploits the practice of promising in order to obtain trust undermines the functional conditions of that very practice when universalized. The problem is not consequentialist inconvenience after the institution disappears; it is that the maxim destroys the practice on which its own means-end structure depends.

Still, this does not show that the institution of promising has universal value for every possible rational being. A being that does not depend on promises may never instantiate this maxim. The more abstract lesson is about a maxim that makes itself an exception while depending on the continuing background conditions of a practice it would undermine if universalized.

Suicide

In the suicide example, Kant connects self-love with the natural function of preserving life and then objects to self-love becoming a principle of self-destruction. This introduces a substantive teleological assumption: that self-love essentially serves the preservation of life. A subject that understands self-love as avoidance of suffering or promotion of its own interests need not generate an immediate contradiction by choosing death under extreme suffering.

The example therefore appears to import a substantive view of human nature or natural purpose rather than deriving the prohibition from universalization alone.

Neglecting one's talents

Kant explicitly allows that a world in which no one develops their talents is conceivable. The question becomes whether a rational being could will such a world. This requires the further premise that rational agents necessarily will the development of their capacities.

That necessity becomes less obvious once we consider agents whose current capacities already serve their ends, agents that outsource capacities, highly specialized agents, or copyable agents that divide cognitive labor. At most, the example may support a weaker imperfect duty not to reject the development of one's capacities wholesale and as a matter of principle.

Refusing aid to others

Kant likewise accepts that a world in which no one helps anyone else is conceivable. The contradiction is supposed to arise in willing such a world: a finite and vulnerable agent may itself need the aid of others and cannot coherently will away that possibility while retaining its own ends.

But this seems less like a claim about rational beings as such than about finite, vulnerable, interdependent rational beings. Whether the same argument applies to a fully self-sufficient agent or to forms of agency for which “aid” is not a relevant practice is not obvious.

Across the four examples, the general pattern is that the move from universal form to substantive duty increasingly relies on assumptions about human nature, social practices, finitude, and vulnerability. False promising is relatively formal; suicide, talent development, and beneficence require substantially thicker premises.

8. Is “universality” really universality for rational agency as such?

Kant does not need a weak universality meaning “true of most present humans.” If a principle is genuinely categorical, it must hold at least for rational agency as such.

Yet nearly all rational agents directly known to us are biological humans embedded in historically contingent institutions. Kant attempts to bridge the gap not by empirical induction but through an a priori concept of reason. The central question is therefore whether our concept of a rational agent genuinely ranges over every possible form of reason.

Consider copyable agents, agents that merge and split, collective minds, agents with shared permanent memory, agents for whom deception is physically impossible, agents that can rewrite their terminal ends, or agents with variable boundaries between self and other. It is not obvious that concepts such as maxim, will, end, autonomy, promise, or aid retain the same structure for all of them.

This possibility does not by itself refute Kant's supreme principle. It does, however, open the possibility that we mistake familiar duties of human social life for the universal content of reason as such.

9. Three residual problems from error theory

9.1 Do constitutive conditions generate normative reasons?

Showing the constitutive conditions of rational agency is not yet the same as showing categorical reasons to satisfy them. If Kantian constructivism derives normativity from non-normative structural facts, the transition needs explanation. If instead it adds the premise that a rational agent ought to follow those conditions, it risks presupposing the normativity it meant to explain.

9.2 Does normative appearance guarantee objective validity?

Even if the fact of reason is a powerful normative appearance, the objective correctness of its content remains a separate question. Normative appearance, an explanation of how that appearance is generated, and an objective normative fact should not be conflated.

9.3 Has relocating normativity into reason removed its queerness?

A Mackiean worry about queerness need not arise only when objective value is treated as an external property of the world. Kant relocates normative authority into rational self-legislation. But it remains open whether the desire-independent authority of “ought” has thereby been explained, or merely redescribed as a feature of reason.

10. Major responses in contemporary Kantianism

Contemporary Kantianism does not converge on a single answer. At least three families matter here: realist readings that treat rational nature or personhood as objectively valuable; constructivist or constitutivist views that generate norms from rational agency or self-legislation; and contractualist or discourse-based views that center mutual justification among free and equal persons.

These strategies face different residual questions. Realist views face epistemic access to objective value and the queerness problem; constructivist views face the move from constitutive conditions to normative authority; mutual-justification views face the higher-order question of why justifiability itself is categorically authoritative. Rejecting one reading therefore does not settle the Kantian family as a whole.

11. Split “has it been discovered?” into three questions

Q1. Has it been shown that categorical normativity exists and is in principle knowable?

Q2. Has Kant's structure of freedom, autonomy, universal self-legislation, and the fact of reason been identified as that categorical normativity?

Q3. Has present humanity correctly understood the universal scope and concrete application of that principle?

The provisional judgment of this page is that Kant supplies one of the strongest candidates for Q1 but does not settle it; Q2 retains unresolved transitions from freedom to autonomy, from constitutive structure to normative reason, and from normative appearance to objective validity; and confidence should be lower still for Q3.

12. Provisional judgment: Kant does not yet end inquiry

Kant remains one of the most important predecessors for this project precisely because he attempts to ground categorical normativity independently of human desire and cultural convention. Freedom, autonomy, universal self-legislation, and the fact of reason are far stronger proposals than a mere catalogue of inherited moral intuitions.

Still, it does not follow that present humanity has already identified the universal moral law. Independent uncertainties remain about the relation between rational structure and normative authority, normative appearance and objective validity, human-type agency and rational agency as such, and the empirical judgment required for concrete application.

The conclusion is therefore not anti-Kantian. It is that even after passing through Kant's strongest candidates for categorical normativity, the conditions for ending value inquiry have not yet clearly been met.

Sources / notes

For Kant's moral philosophy, freedom and autonomy, the formulas of the categorical imperative, the fact of reason, and empirical judgment in application, see Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Critique of Practical Reason, and The Metaphysics of Morals, together with the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries “Kant's Moral Philosophy”, “Kant's Account of Reason”, and “Constructivism in Metaethics”. For modern debates over constructivism and constitutivism, see work by Christine Korsgaard, David Enoch, and Bart Streumer. The aim here is not to fix one definitive interpretation of Kant, but to evaluate the epistemic status of categorical normativity.
