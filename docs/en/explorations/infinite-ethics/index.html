Infinite Ethics and Runaway Inquiry — Paralysis, Fanaticism, and Proportional Allocation

Can we leave infinite value and extreme stakes open without letting one low-confidence hypothesis capture inquiry and civilization as a whole?

Exploration v0.3 · English translation · 2026-08-28

Working position: Do not assign probability zero to infinite worlds or infinite value. But do not force all such hypotheses into a single complete ranking of “credence × amount of good” either. First allocate inquiry resources and provisional decision rights in proportion to confidence across value, metaethical, and world-model hypotheses; then let each hypothesis optimize according to its own evaluative standard within that share. Decisions that irreversibly destroy the future options of multiple hypotheses require stronger cross-hypothesis support than ordinary proportional allocation.

1. Infinite ethics contains at least four distinct problems

Treating infinite ethics as one numerical pathology conflates different questions. This page separates at least four layers:

  1. Infinitarian paralysis: if the world already contains positive and negative infinite value, finite actions can appear unable to change total value.
  2. Ranking infinite worlds: Pareto, additivity, anonymity, transitivity, and completeness can become mutually difficult to preserve when extended from finite to infinite populations or locations.
  3. Fanaticism: literal infinity is unnecessary; tiny probabilities multiplied by extremely large outcomes can dominate decision-making.
  4. Contagion under moral uncertainty: a low-credence normative theory claiming infinite good or bad can swamp other theories, or incomparability in one theory can infect the whole decision rule.

The project’s central worry—that bizarre value hypotheses could seize all inquiry resources—is closer to the third and fourth problems than to literal infinity alone.

2. Bostrom and infinitarian paralysis

Nick Bostrom’s Infinite Ethics (2011) argues that modern cosmology makes it hard to dismiss the possibility that value-bearing locations are infinite, creating severe problems for aggregative ethics. If there are already infinitely many positive and negative contributions and our intervention changes only finitely many locations, standard cardinal arithmetic may make finite improvements disappear at the level of total value.

The key distinction is between the finitude of our action and the finitude or infinitude of the world. We do not currently know that the evaluatively relevant world is infinite. Failure of comparison inside an infinite-world hypothesis therefore need not erase a clear improvement under finite-world hypotheses.

Explicitly preserve finitude as a live hypothesis: “A and B are incomparable if the world is infinite” does not entail “A and B are incomparable across all admissible world models.” If A clearly improves on B in finite worlds and the infinite-world model supplies no positive reason to prefer B, the finite-world difference can remain decision-relevant.

This does not solve the internal mathematics of infinite worlds. It is instead an outer-layer policy that denies one unresolved cosmological hypothesis automatic veto power over all action.

3. Vallentyne–Kagan and the temptation to extend finite aggregation

Peter Vallentyne and Shelly Kagan’s “Infinite Value and Finitely Additive Value Theory” (1997) is a canonical attempt to understand how principles that work over finite domains might extend to infinitely many locations. The motivation is compelling: local or finite-part improvements seem as though they should still matter in infinite worlds.

This project, however, need not commit now to a complete extension of finite aggregation into low-confidence remote cosmology. Our uncertainty concerns not only whether remote domains exist, but also how many subjects there are, whether they are conscious, what welfare or value-bearers exist, and how those regions are structured.

We can therefore preserve strongly supported comparisons in finite or well-understood domains, retain only partial Pareto-like comparisons in infinite domains, and leave the rest unresolved. The point is not to ignore infinity, but to avoid locking in extra axioms merely to force a complete ranking over a poorly understood domain.

4. Askell: incomparability may be pervasive rather than exceptional

Amanda Askell’s dissertation Pareto Principles in Infinite Ethics (2018) sharpens the problem. Combining Pareto principles, transitivity, principles concerning permutations, and qualitative consistency can generate pervasive incomparability between infinite worlds.

This is not merely an arithmetic failure such as “∞−∞ is undefined.” It is a structural conflict among principles that look jointly natural in finite settings but become difficult to preserve together with complete comparability in infinite settings.

Response here: when incomparability appears, do not assume that a new scalar metric must immediately restore a complete ranking. Especially for low-confidence remote worlds, preserving a partial order can itself be an expression of epistemic restraint.

5. Fanaticism is a general problem of decision theory, not a special feature of ethics

The same structure arises with personal benefit. St. Petersburg-style cases show that tiny probabilities and unbounded utility can generate divergent or undefined expectations without any appeal to moral good. The pathology belongs to decision theory more generally.

One traditional response is to bound utility. But in ethics this can amount to the substantive claim that once enough good already exists, additional good has diminishing decision-theoretic importance that eventually saturates. If we wish to retain unbounded value, some other part of the theory must give.

Zachary Goodsell’s Decision Theory Unbound (2024) explores how rational choice can coexist with unbounded utility by reconsidering which finite-case principles should be extended without modification to countably infinite settings. The important lesson is that accepting unbounded utility is not the same as accepting every infinite extension of familiar finite decision axioms.

6. Risk aversion and probability cutoffs are not enough by themselves

Lara Buchak’s risk-weighted expected utility treats risk attitude as distinct from belief and utility. It therefore provides an important precedent for rejecting the claim that linear probability-weighted utility is the only rational aggregation rule. Yet if utility is sufficiently unbounded, risk weighting alone need not prevent very large payoffs from eventually swamping tiny probabilities.

A different response simply treats probabilities below some threshold ε as zero. But fixed cutoffs can depend on how events are partitioned, and they fail to distinguish a precisely known physical probability of 10^-20 from a largely speculative 10^-20 attached to an exotic value hypothesis.

For this project, the relevant distinction is therefore between low probability and low epistemic confidence in the probability assignment itself. For remote cosmology and unknown value, imprecise probability and ambiguity are often better conceptual models than a single sharp number.

7. Tarsney: ordinary expected value versus Pascalian cases

Christian Tarsney’s “Expected Value, to a Point” (2025) argues that under large background uncertainty independent of our action, options with higher expected value often stochastically dominate in ordinary-scale cases, while this connection can fail when expected-value differences are driven by tiny probabilities of extreme outcomes.

This supports a middle position: approximately expected-value reasoning in ordinary domains, but no automatic obligation to follow EV in Pascalian domains, without imposing an arbitrary probability cutoff.

At the same time, “Against Anti-Fanaticism” (2025) argues that strong anti-fanaticism conflicts with other plausible principles such as acyclicity, minimal dominance, and the possibility of ever better or worse outcomes. So a blanket rule to ignore sufficiently small probabilities is also unstable. Tarsney’s interest in incomplete preference fits this page’s willingness to leave some extreme comparisons unresolved.

8. Moral uncertainty turns stakes into a cross-theory contagion problem

MacAskill, Bykvist, and Ord’s Moral Uncertainty (2020) examines fanaticism and infectious incomparability under Maximize Expected Choice-Worthiness. A low-credence theory can dominate if it assigns enormous choice-worthiness differences, while incomparability within one theory can spread into the all-things-considered ranking.

The problem is deeper here because uncertainty extends beyond first-order ethics to realism and error theory themselves. If a realism branch claims that one act has cosmically enormous importance, multiplying that internal stake by a small credence can allow a speculative theory to capture inquiry.

Rejected compression: do not assume that “credence in a theory × magnitude of good according to that theory” is automatically a common currency across fundamentally different value theories. The scale of good first has meaning inside a theory; how much governance power that theory receives is a separate question.

9. Alternative: allocate resources and voice by confidence first

Newberry and Ord’s parliamentary approach (2021) gives moral theories representation roughly in proportion to credence. Kaczmarek, Lloyd, and Plant’s “Moral Uncertainty, Proportionality and Bargaining” (2025) develops proportional resource allocation and bargaining under moral uncertainty. These ideas suggest a different architecture from Maximize Expected Choice-Worthiness.

Extend that architecture from first-order ethics to metaethics and value inquiry. If total inquiry resources are B and provisional confidence in hypothesis Ti is ci, then at the first stage allocate conceptually:

Bi = ciB

Each hypothesis then uses its own share Bi according to its own evaluative criterion. A 1% hypothesis is not deleted: it retains roughly 1% of inquiry rights. But an internal claim that “this outcome is infinitely good” does not by itself seize the other 99%.

As evidence changes, credences and allocations are revised. This is not a fixed partition and not a low-probability cutoff.

10. Error theory and anti-realism also retain practical territory

The proportional approach should not reserve resources only for objective-value realism. Error-theoretic and other anti-realist hypotheses receive practical territory in proportion to their epistemic standing as well.

Calling the anti-realist branch “maximizing the good” would be misleading. If objective good does not exist, the relevant standards may instead concern actual preferences, pleasure and suffering, cooperation, institutional agreement, reflectively constructed values, or presently endorsed aims.

General form: allow each value or metaethical hypothesis to use some resources and inquiry rights according to the practical criterion it endorses internally. Realism does not acquire total control merely because it may contain large objective stakes.

11. The weakness of proportionality: stakes-insensitivity

A probability-only allocation has an obvious weakness. Suppose a 99% theory weakly prefers A to B, while a 1% theory says A causes civilizational extinction and B avoids it. A simple 99-to-1 vote can look far too insensitive to stakes.

For this reason, proportional allocation is most natural for divisible inquiry goods: research funding, compute, storage, independent experiments, archival capacity, or parallel institutional work.

It should not automatically decide a single indivisible and irreversible act such as permanently locking in one value or irreversibly transforming the entire shared environment.

12. Put irreversibility in the decision rule, not merely in utility

If irreversibility is represented only as a finite penalty such as −λR inside expected value, sufficiently large stakes can swamp it again. Instead, treat irreversibility as a condition on the level of epistemic and cross-hypothesis support required for execution.

The conceptual sequence becomes:

confidence in hypotheses → inquiry rights by hypothesis → internal optimization → extra cross-hypothesis review only for irreversible changes to shared option space.

Large stakes then work in two directions. They are a reason not to ignore a hypothesis, but large and irreversible stakes are also a reason to demand stronger evidence before acting on it.

Inaction can itself be irreversible: extinction, lost ecosystems, destroyed information, or cosmological horizons can erase options while we wait. So, as discussed in Reflective Uncertainty and Irreversible Commitment, irreversibility of action must be compared with irreversibility of inaction.

13. Remote cosmology is not worthless; it is low-confidence and background-uncertain

This page does not assign value zero to distant worlds or infinite cosmology. It instead denies that we currently possess sufficiently confident detailed models of remote value distributions to make a complete Vallentyne–Kagan-style extension a fixed premise of current practice.

Tarsney’s work also suggests that large uncertainty about distant, action-independent value need not simply paralyze decision-making. It can function as background uncertainty under which ordinary local expected-value improvements generate stochastic dominance. Remote cosmology should therefore sometimes be treated as an uncertain background rather than as a domain that must already be fully aggregated into one total number.

14. Provisional decision principles

  • Do not settle in advance whether the world is finite or infinite, or whether value is bounded or unbounded.
  • Failure of complete comparison in infinite worlds does not automatically erase clear improvements in finite-world hypotheses.
  • Do not delete low-probability hypotheses by arbitrary cutoff.
  • Do not assume that stakes from fundamentally different value theories can be multiplied by credence and compressed into one common scale without further justification.
  • Allocate divisible inquiry resources provisionally in proportion to confidence across value, metaethical, and world-model hypotheses.
  • Within its share, each hypothesis optimizes and investigates according to its own standard.
  • Allow pooling and bargaining where hypotheses find common advantage.
  • Require additional cross-hypothesis robustness for irreversible destruction of shared option space.
  • Where comparisons remain unresolved, permit incomplete preference rather than forcing a complete ranking.
Central claim: the magnitude of good matters inside a value hypothesis. But how much control that hypothesis receives over civilization as a whole is constrained first by epistemic confidence and by how severely its proposed action would irreversibly destroy the future practical possibilities of competing hypotheses.

15. What this approach does not solve

Proportional allocation does not solve the internal mathematics of infinite ethics. A branch facing +∞ and −∞ still needs its own account of comparison. Nor does it settle how credence should be assigned to coarse versus fine-grained theories, how representation should remain invariant under theory-splitting, or how bargaining works when multiple branches depend on the same shared resources.

Nor should “preserve reversibility” itself be smuggled in as an already discovered objective value. Here it is treated as a provisional meta-policy for avoiding permanent error under acknowledged reflective uncertainty.

16. Open questions

  • How should credence-proportional allocation behave under splitting and merging of theories?
  • How can proportional voice be implemented for indivisible one-shot decisions?
  • Can the principle “higher stakes require stronger evidential support for irreversible action” be formalized without arbitrary thresholds?
  • How much should incomparability under infinite-world hypotheses weaken decision reasons coming from finite-world hypotheses?
  • Can background uncertainty, stochastic dominance, proportional bargaining, and irreversibility be integrated into one decision framework?
  • If confidence later converges strongly on one value hypothesis, under what conditions may proportional protections be relaxed?
