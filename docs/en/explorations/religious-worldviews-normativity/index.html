Do Religious Worldviews Generate Normativity? — Decomposing World-Models, Sanctions, Goodness, and Authority

religion · theology · divine command · apophaticism · Exploration v0.1 · 2026-09-01

Working position: Religious possibilities should not be excluded from the hypothesis space. But the truth of a world-model involving gods, spirits, rebirth, or revelation does not by itself establish goodness, normative authority, obligation, or worship-worthiness. Religious packages should be decomposed by claim type, with each inferential arrow requiring independent evidence and a defensible bridge.

1. “Is religion true?” is too coarse a question

Religion is not one proposition. This page provisionally separates descriptive world-model claims D, hypothetical reasons derived from existing goals H, normative claims about value, reasons, obligations, and fittingness N, expressive and symbolic practices E, and institutional practices I. Rather than drawing one border between the institutions called “science” and “religion,” the priority is to avoid conflating claim types.

Gould’s NOMA captures an important fact/value distinction but poorly handles the factual content of resurrection, creation, miracles, and afterlife claims, while normativity is not the exclusive domain of religion. Dawkins rightly returns religious D-claims to ordinary competition among world-models, but the failure of D does not automatically refute expressive or institutional practices. Dennett’s naturalization of religion can explain causal origins of belief without thereby settling truth or normativity.

2. Provisional position on religious world-models

On present evidence, detailed supernatural models proposed by existing religions do not appear to outperform naturalistic explanations as best explanations. Revelation, miracle reports, religious experience, and transmission are weakened as strong evidence by limited independent physical and historical support, mutually incompatible testimony, culture-sensitive interpretation, and plausible natural cognitive and social pathways.

Yet genealogy is an undercutter, not a deductive refutation: explaining why a belief arose does not entail that its content is false. The project can therefore assign low epistemic weight to detailed existing religious hypotheses while refusing to assign zero prior weight to unconceived religious or supernatural possibilities. This follows the same non-exclusion principle used in open-world moral uncertainty and open hypothesis spaces.

3. Early religion can concern effective powers rather than good beings

Rainmaking, fertility rites, healing, appeasement, and ancestor rites often fit D + G → R_h(A). If a deity controls rain and one wants rain, prayer may be instrumentally rational. If hell exists and one wants to avoid it, obedience may carry an enormous hypothetical reason. None of this requires the deity to be good.

Japanese myth and Shinto provide a useful contrast to perfect-being theology. Divinity does not entail moral perfection; aramitama/nigimitama, pacification of dangerous spirits, and transformation of vengeful spirits into protective kami illustrate how one power may appear as both calamity and benefit. Divinity ≠ Power ≠ MoralGoodness.

4. Sanction is not normativity

Religions have long used reward and punishment as motives, while Plato, Christian critiques of servile fear, Shaftesbury, and Kant all develop versions of the demand that a moral reason survive removal of reward and sanction.

If a god can certainly send dissenters to hell, that may generate an extremely strong Rh. But R_h(A) ⇒ R_n(A) does not follow. J. S. Mill’s willingness to accept hell rather than call an immoral ruler good sharply illustrates the distinction; Don Giovanni offers a literary case of refusing repentance even with damnation at stake.

5. Huck Finn — internalized conscience still does not guarantee truth

The famous scene in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn is not merely a calculation about avoiding hell. Huck sincerely judges helping Jim to escape as morally wrong because he has internalized the surrounding social and religious order. Yet sympathy for Jim moves him to act against that judgment.

The deeper issue is therefore that even when sanctions become internalized as categorical conscience, the whole package social learning → Ĉ + B̂ + R̂ may still be false. From Huck’s own standpoint this resembles akrasia; from a later moral standpoint it can be read as inverse akrasia. The appearance of unconditional bindingness and the objective correctness of the represented norm must remain distinct.

6. Transforming goal G becomes a value claim when the transformation is demanded

Religions often do more than supply means to present goals: they command agents to abandon some desires, love God, renounce attachment, or become a different kind of person. If a practice merely causes G0 → G1, that is a descriptive psychological claim. If a current goal instrumentally supports acquiring G1, that is H. But the claim that one ought to become an agent with G1 rather than G0 is a genuine C/B/R claim about value.

7. How does theology get from C to B?

Even if “God is good,” “mercy is good,” or “holiness is valuable” supplies C, reasons and obligations do not automatically follow. Theological traditions have located the bridge in divine command, natural law, divine nature, creation relations, practical reason, or rightful authority.

Divine-command theory proposes something like Command_G(A) → Obligation(A); natural-law theory links goods and human nature to practical reason; Adams-style views separate resemblance-to-God accounts of goodness from divine-command accounts of obligation. These remain candidate bridges B. The project asks one step further: why is the bridge itself true?

8. Does God create reasons, or communicate them?

“God says do A” can perform at least four different roles.

  1. Epistemic communication: God knows an independently existing Rn(A) and tells us about it.
  2. Evidence: God’s testimony raises credence in Rn(A), provided divine reliability is independently supported.
  3. Reason-generation: the command itself constitutes Rn(A), as in genuine divine-command or authority theories.
  4. Sanction: reward or punishment for compliance generates Rh(A).

Conflating these lets omniscience, goodness, authority, and coercive power slide into a single undifferentiated “reason to obey.” Each route requires a separate justification.

9. How is divine goodness recognized?

Major routes include revelation, perfect-being theology, metaphysical convertibility of being and goodness, inference from creation or providence, moral reason independent of God, and religious experience. Each faces a different pressure: circularity, definitional loading, the bridge from ontological to moral goodness, selective use of observations, or the reliability of moral intuition.

If “God” is defined as omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good, perfect goodness is analytically built in. But the substantive question remains whether an actual creator or ultimate being instantiates that concept. Creator ≠ PerfectGoodGod must remain a live distinction.

10. Apophatic theology — can unknown goodness save theology?

Pseudo-Dionysius, Maimonides, and other apophatic traditions deny that ordinary human predicates straightforwardly capture the divine essence. This epistemic humility partly resembles the project’s insistence that V̂_human ≠ V*.

Yet the more divine goodness is detached from human concepts of goodness, the more resistant it becomes to the problem of evil while also losing inferential content for Good(G) → Trust(G), obedience, or worship. Increasing unknowability can increase resistance to refutation while simultaneously decreasing discriminatory power.

11. Extending the Evil-God Challenge to alien value

Stephen Law’s Evil-God Challenge asks why a perfectly evil god should be less reasonable than a perfectly good god if symmetrical theodicies can be constructed. Responses appeal to skeptical theism, privation accounts of evil, asymmetries between rational agency and goodness, or pragmatic benefits of good-god belief.

This project broadens the comparison: a humanly recognizable good god G+, a recognizable evil god G−, a god pursuing unknown objective value G*, a god pursuing unknown anti-value G¬*, and a value-indifferent creator G0. A genuine Good/Evil asymmetry is not ruled out. But claims such as Being → Good or RationalAgency → Good are themselves substantive bridges requiring independent support.

12. Evil and divine hiddenness become identification problems

If true goodness may be radically alien to present human value, apparent suffering and divine hiddenness need not logically exclude every perfectly good divine being. Perfect goodness may not entail welfare maximization or the pursuit of explicit personal relationship.

But this escape has an epistemic price. Strange goodness may preserve the possibility of a perfectly good god without preserving our ability to identify one. The question shifts from “why would a good god allow this?” to “what evidence distinguishes a good god from an evil god, an indifferent creator, or a deity pursuing alien value?”

13. Worship, sacredness, and fittingness should also be decomposed

A psychological desire to worship may be a locally produced preference shaped by evolution, culture, and socialization. By contrast, “this being is worthy of worship” is a fittingness claim and returns to the foundational problem developed in Reasons First and normative structure. A sacred place can similarly involve D (“a deity appeared here”), E/I (“this community treats it as special”), and Fn (“awe or reverence is objectively fitting here”).

Existence(G) ≠ WorshipWorthiness(G); worship-worthiness need not be admitted as an unexplained primitive.

14. False religious fictions may still be useful

Even if a religious D-claim is false, ritual, myth, commemoration, or common symbolism may support trust, coordination, and long-term commitment. False(D) ∧ Useful(E/I) is coherent.

For an inquiry community, however, transparent fiction differs from enforcing falsehood as fact. The latter can destroy epistemic trust and thereby reduce long-run inquiry capacity.

15. Non-theistic traditions reveal different connections

Buddhism strongly combines descriptive claims about suffering, karma, rebirth, and liberation with hypothetical reasons for agents who seek liberation; once it claims that suffering ought to be eliminated or that agents ought to transform into bodhisattva-like agents, an additional value bridge is required. Confucian traditions offer different epistemologies through Mencian moral sprouts, Xunzian social technology, Zhu Xi’s li, and Wang Yangming’s liangzhi. Zhuangzi can instead be read as a predecessor of anti-lock-in toward current evaluative representations.

Even a world-model such as Ragnarök can be assigned extremely low present epistemic weight without being assigned literal zero. If evidence changed, hypothetical practice could change; the further claim that preparing for battle is intrinsically good would remain a distinct N-claim.

16. Provisional conclusion — keep religious possibilities open, inspect each arrow

The project does not exclude divine beings, revelation, divine command, sacredness, ancestor rites, or unknown religious value from the hypothesis space. But non-exclusion from the hypothesis space grants neither practical privilege nor veto power.

Architecture: separate, where possible, descriptive world-model D; goals and preferences G/P; value content C; bridge principles B; reasons and obligations R; fittingness F; and cultural/institutional practice E/I.
Existence(G) ⇏ Good(G) ⇏ Authority(G) ⇏ Obligation ⇏ Worship-worthiness.
If a genuinely irreducible normative structure remains after decomposition, add it as a new open-world candidate and compare it under the same epistemic standards as every other candidate.
